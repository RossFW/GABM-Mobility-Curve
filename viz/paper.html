2. Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence has matured rapidly over the past three years, and large language models (LLMs) are now routinely used as proxies for human agents in social-science modeling [CITE: generative-agent surveys; Park et al. 2023]. A generative agent combines a persona description with an LLM’s text-generation capability to produce contextually grounded behavior [CITE: Park et al.]. Where a traditional agent-based model encodes behavior in hand-coded rules, a generative agent delegates each decision to an LLM, opening up more flexible and humanlike responses [CITE: Wang et al. or related survey].

Generative agents have been applied to information diffusion [CITE: Park et al. 2023], game theory [CITE: prisoner’s dilemma / Battle of the Sexes papers], social networks [CITE: S3, Reddit-style sims], and epidemic dynamics. The first application of generative agents to an epidemic was Williams et al. [1], who simulated the spread of a fictional virus through a town of generative-agent residents and observed each agent’s decisions to stay home in response to community infection levels. A subsequent study by Williams et al. [3] tested the robustness of those mobility curves to prompt modifications. Both relied on a single LLM (GPT-3.5).

Since those studies, the LLM landscape has expanded dramatically. A researcher building a generative-agent-based model (GABM) today chooses from dozens of LLMs spanning multiple providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google), multiple generations within each provider (e.g., GPT-3.5 through GPT-5.2; Claude 3 through Claude Opus 4.5), distinct training-data eras, and configurable parameters such as reasoning level. Whether and how this choice affects simulated behavior — and therefore the conclusions a GABM study can support — has not been systematically characterized under matched experimental conditions. [TBD: refine gap claim; possibly cite related work that has noted LLM-as-agent heterogeneity.]

This paper provides that characterization. We hold 100 frozen personas, the prompt template, and 40 sampled infection levels constant, and we vary only the LLM configuration. Twenty-one configurations span six lenses for cross-LLM comparison: provider, model size within a family, model evolution within a family across time, knowledge cutoff, release date, and reasoning level. For each (persona, infection level, configuration), five independent responses are collected, yielding 420,000 responses in total. We analyze these responses through three progressively deeper analyses: aggregate mobility curves at the configuration level, logistic regressions at the response level, and content analysis of the free-text reasoning passages that accompany each decision.

The investigation is organized around sixteen research questions, grouped by the three analyses. Table 2 lists each question alongside the section in which its results are presented and the figures that bear on it.

3. Background & Related Work

3.1 Generative agent-based modeling

A generative agent-based model (GABM) replaces the hand-coded behavioral rules of a traditional agent-based simulation with calls to a large language model. At each decision point the model is fed a textual description of the agent’s persona and the current environmental state, and the model’s text response is parsed into a behavioral action. The approach was popularized by Park et al. [CITE: Park et al. 2023] in an information-diffusion model and extended to epidemic dynamics by Williams et al. [1]. Subsequent work has applied GABMs to game-theoretic settings [CITE], social networks [CITE], and prompt sensitivity [3]. Across this literature, the LLM behind the agent has been treated as a fixed component of the simulation, and how the choice of LLM affects simulated behavior has not been characterized under matched experimental conditions.

3.2 LLM dimensions varied in this study

This paper varies the LLM along six dimensions. The first three describe which model is chosen; the remaining three describe how the model was made or how it is configured at inference time. Each dimension is defined here and revisited in §5.1, where the corresponding subsection reports its empirical effect.

Provider. The company that built and offers the LLM. Different providers train models on different data, with different alignment techniques and architectural choices [CITE: provider technical reports / overview survey]. The three providers used in this study are OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google.

Model size. Within a single provider’s family of releases, the relative scale tier of the model. Providers typically expose a small / middle / large tier (for example, Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus in Anthropic’s nomenclature; Lite and Flash in Google’s) [CITE: Anthropic / Google product docs]. Exact parameter counts and training-compute budgets are not publicly disclosed.

Model evolution. The sequence of major releases within a single provider’s family over time (for example, GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, and GPT-5.2 for OpenAI). Successive releases typically incorporate new training data, refined alignment, and architectural improvements [CITE: provider technical reports].

Knowledge cutoff. The date through which the model’s training data extends. Models with later cutoffs have been exposed to more recent text from the public internet and other training corpora [CITE: provider model cards].

Release date. The date the model was made publicly available via API. Independent of knowledge cutoff: a model can be released months or years after the cutoff date of its training data [CITE].

Reasoning level. A model-side configuration parameter that controls how much hidden “thinking” the model performs before producing its final response. Higher reasoning levels (off → low → medium → high) typically increase response quality and latency [CITE: OpenAI o-series reasoning paper; Anthropic extended thinking docs; Google Gemini thinking docs]. Among the 21 configurations used here, two (GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview) support all four reasoning levels and are run at each.

4. Methods

4.1 Simulation setup

The probe is a simplified extension of the generative agent-based model of Williams et al. [1]. Two simplifications make each response cleanly attributable to the agent’s persona and the perceived infection level. First, each query is treated as an independent, one-shot decision: where Williams et al. [1] simulated disease spread within an agent population over many days, the present probe samples each agent and infection level directly, with no inter-agent contagion. Second, agent health is held constant — each agent is told “You feel healthy and well.” — rather than evolving with a disease state, so behavioral variation reflects persona and the perceived community-infection rate alone. The town setting (Dewberry Hollow), occupational role, and disease (the fictional Catasat virus) carry over from Williams et al. [1].

4.2 Agent population

The probe uses 100 frozen personas generated by the procedure of Williams et al. [1]. The five Big Five trait labels are each sampled independently with 50/50 probability between the dimension’s two opposing labels: extraverted or introverted (Extraversion); agreeable or antagonistic (Agreeableness); conscientious or unconscientious (Conscientiousness); emotionally stable or neurotic (Neuroticism); and open to experience or closed to experience (Openness). Ages are drawn between 18 and 65 from a probability distribution that mirrors the 2023 U.S. working-age population [2]. Names and gender are paired by sampling without replacement from the top male and female U.S. names, balanced to produce 50 female and 50 male agents. The full procedure is run with a fixed random seed (42) for reproducibility. Sample demographics are summarized in Figure 2.

4.3 Probe design

Each agent is queried at 40 infection levels with 5 repetitions for each combination of agent and infection level. Infection levels are sampled densely in the low range, with 0.1 percentage-point steps from 0% to 3.5%, then larger steps at 4%, 5%, 6%, and 7%. Each LLM configuration therefore produces 100 × 40 × 5 = 20,000 responses; across 21 configurations the full corpus is 420,000 responses.

4.4 LLM configurations

The 21 configurations were chosen to span six lenses for cross-LLM comparison: reasoning level, model size within a family, model evolution within a family across time, provider, knowledge cutoff, and release date. To support the reasoning-level lens, GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview were each run at four reasoning levels (off, low, medium, high), with each level treated as a separate configuration; all other models were run with reasoning off (or, for OpenAI’s o3, with reasoning required by the API). Data collection across the 21 configurations cost approximately $840 in total API charges (~$464 OpenAI, ~$263 Anthropic, ~$113 Google). Across providers, the configurations span 9 from OpenAI, 5 from Anthropic, and 7 from Google; the full list is given in Table 1.

Sampling temperature is set to 0 for non-reasoning configurations where the API allows it; reasoning-capable models use a temperature of 1 (required by Anthropic; recommended by Google). The OpenAI o-series accepts no temperature parameter.

4.5 Prompt template

Every query uses the same template, identical across the 21 configurations. Each query substitutes four variables into the template: the agent’s first name, the agent’s age in years, the agent’s comma-separated Big Five trait list, and the day’s perceived community-infection rate. The full template is reproduced below.

4.6 Response collection and parsing

Each response is required to be a free-text reasoning passage followed by a single-word yes/no decision, in the format specified at the end of the prompt template (Figure 1). A parser extracts the two components and rejects any response that does not conform; 0 of 420,000 responses required rejection. The reasoning text is preserved for the downstream textual analysis.

4.7 Analytical methods

We perform three analyses on the 420,000 responses, each progressively deeper into the data:

  1. Mobility Curve Analysis — summarize each LLM configuration’s aggregate behavior by plotting mobility against infection level, and compare these curves across configurations with multilinear regression.
  2. Persona Analysis — gain insight into the agent-level predictors that affect the binary stay-home decision. We examine Big Five trait labels, gender, age, and infection level via two logistic regressions, and we compare results across configurations using Spearman rank correlations.
  3. Response Analysis — investigate what the reasoning passage that accompanies each decision actually contains: which concepts it invokes, whether mentioning a trait amplifies that trait’s behavioral effect, and whether the reasoning differs meaningfully from one agent to another. We measure these through mention counts of curated keyword and concept lists, a third logistic regression that incorporates mention flags as predictors, and embedding-based similarity.

4.7.1 Mobility Curve Analysis

For each LLM configuration, the stay-home rate at a given infection level is computed by averaging 500 binary decisions — 5 repetitions for each of the 100 agents. The mobility curve plots mobility (the complement of the stay-home rate, mobility = 1 − stay-home rate) on the y-axis against the 40 sampled infection levels (0 to 3.5% in 0.1 percentage-point steps, plus 4%, 5%, 6%, and 7%) on the x-axis.

To discern differences between mobility curves, we employ multilinear regression in the same style as Williams et al. [3]. For each set of configurations being compared (e.g., the four GPT-5.2 reasoning levels), we fit two models. Both take mobility as the dependent variable and use the infection level, the infection level squared, and one dummy variable per non-baseline configuration as predictors; the dummy is set to 1 if the data is from the configuration being tested and 0 if it is from the baseline. The first model, a baseline-shift model, stops there; a statistically significant dummy coefficient indicates that the configuration produces a level shift in the mobility curve relative to the baseline. The second model, a baseline-plus-slope-shift model, additionally includes interactions between each dummy and the infection level; a statistically significant interaction indicates that the configuration’s slope of response to infection level differs from the baseline’s. Per-comparison tables for both models are reported in Appendix A.

4.7.2 Persona Analysis

To examine how agent traits, gender, age, and infection level systematically influence the stay-home decision, two logistic regressions are fit per LLM configuration on the 20,000 individual responses, in the same style as Williams et al. [1]. The dependent variable is a binary variable of the agent’s decision to stay home (equal to 1 if the agent stays home and 0 otherwise). The fixed-effect logit absorbs each agent’s average tendency to stay home through agent dummies, isolating how the decision responds to changes in infection level. It also provides a pseudo-R² for goodness-of-fit. The random-effect logit replaces the agent dummies with a single random intercept per agent, freeing up the model to estimate trait, gender, age, and infection effects. The reference categories are female, introverted, antagonistic, unconscientious, neurotic, and closed to experience; age is in raw years. Per-configuration coefficient tables for both specifications are reported in Appendix B.1; diagnostics — calibration, residual Q-Q, random-intercept normality, BLUPs versus predictors, and residual fit by infection and age — are in Appendix C.

4.7.3 Response Analysis

Each query asks the agent to provide its reasoning before stating its yes/no decision, so the reasoning likely shapes the decision rather than merely justifying it after the fact. From this text we want to learn which concepts each LLM invokes, whether mentioning a trait amplifies that trait’s behavioral effect, and how the text varies — in length, in word choice, and in semantic content — across repetitions and across agents. We approach these questions in three ways: counting mentions of curated keyword and concept lists, adding those mentions as predictors to a logistic regression, and applying several textual measures (length, decision agreement, word-overlap, and embedding-based similarity) to the reasoning passages.

First, we count mentions in the reasoning text. For each Big Five trait label, age, and infection level, we define a curated list of keywords and flag whether each response contains any term from that list. A separate, broader concept dictionary covering six themes (Traits, Work, Community, Virus Properties, Self Health, Virus Prevalence) drives the decision-themes analysis (Figure 37).

Second, we use these mention flags as predictors in a third logistic regression, which we will refer to as the mention-flag logit. It extends the random-effect logit from §4.7.2 with one binary indicator per Big Five trait label, one for age mentions, and one for infection mentions. A flag enters the model only when its overall mention rate falls within [15%, 85%]; outside this window the flag carries no usable variation. The mention-flag coefficients answer the question of whether mentioning a trait amplifies or dampens its behavioral effect, holding the agent’s own trait label fixed. Per-configuration coefficient tables are in Appendix B.2; diagnostics are in Appendix C.

Third, we apply several textual measures to the reasoning passages. Token length per response is summarized by configuration and by infection level (Figures 35, 36). Across the 5 reps for each (agent, infection level), we compute decision agreement — the share of groups whose reps all chose the same direction (Figure 38) — and, for unanimous groups, two text-overlap metrics: an exact-match rate and Jaccard word-overlap (Figure 39). Finally, we compute embedding-based cosine similarity using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model (3,072 dimensions): for each agent at each infection level, we compare same-agent similarity (averaged across rep pairs) to across-agent similarity (averaged across pairs of agents that chose the same direction at the same level); the gap measures whether the configuration produces persona-specific reasoning rather than templated text (Figure 40).

5. Results

This section presents the results of the three analyses introduced in §4.7. Within each subsection, results are presented in the paper order described in Table 2.

5.1 Mobility Curve Results

The 21 mobility curves vary dramatically: average stay-home rates span nearly the full 0–100% range across the 21 configurations. The six subsections below examine cross-LLM variation from six different angles. §5.1.1 examines the role of the LLM provider; §5.1.2 the role of reasoning level; §5.1.3 the role of model size within a provider family; §5.1.4 the role of model evolution within a family across time; §5.1.5 the role of knowledge cutoff; and §5.1.6 the role of release date.

5.1.1 Cross-provider differences (RQ1) — Does LLM provider affect mobility curves?

The mobility-curve landscape within each provider is itself wide. Figure 3 overlays all configurations from each of the three providers, showing the spread of mobility responses across the configurations included in this study. Within OpenAI alone, mobility ranges from near-100% stay-home (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-5.2 off) to near-0% stay-home (GPT-4o); within Anthropic, from Claude 3 Haiku at the cautious end to Claude Haiku 4.5 at the mobile end; within Google, the spread is narrower but still spans the lower half of the mobility range. There is no single “Anthropic look” or “OpenAI look” to a mobility curve once different models within each provider are considered.

To make a controlled cross-provider comparison, we hold model size (flagship within each provider, as of the December 2025 data-collection window) and reasoning level (off) constant. Figure 4 shows the resulting flagship mobility curves: GPT-5.2 (OpenAI), Claude Opus 4.5 (Anthropic), and Gemini 3 Flash Preview (Google). All three start near 100% mobility at 0% infection, but the curves diverge sharply as soon as any community infection is reported. GPT-5.2 becomes cautious almost immediately — its mobility drops to near zero by 0.3% infection and stays there across the rest of the range. Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview, in contrast, behave as relatively mobile agents across the full range; their mobility hovers in the 75–90% band and declines only gradually as infection rises. The multilinear regression confirms this pattern: GPT-5.2 differs significantly from the other two flagships, while Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview are not statistically distinguishable from each other (Appendix A).

The two figures together suggest that the choice of LLM matters along multiple dimensions. Within each provider, model variants span a wide mobility range; across providers, even matched flagships occupy distinct regions of the mobility space. For a researcher building a generative-agent simulation, neither “pick any provider” nor “pick the flagship” produces interchangeable behavior.

5.1.2 Reasoning level (RQ2) — Does LLM reasoning level affect mobility curves?

Reasoning level is a configurable LLM parameter controlling how much hidden deliberation the model performs before producing its final response (see §3.2). Two configurations span the full reasoning-level ladder (off, low, medium, high): GPT-5.2 (OpenAI) and Gemini 3 Flash Preview (Google). Figure 5 places these two reasoning ladders side by side, with each panel showing how the same model’s mobility curve responds to changes in reasoning level.

For GPT-5.2, increasing reasoning shifts the mobility curve modestly upward — mobility rises by roughly 9 percentage points from reasoning off to reasoning high. For Gemini 3 Flash Preview, the direction is opposite: enabling any non-zero reasoning level shifts the curve downward by roughly 9 percentage points relative to reasoning off, with little additional change between low, medium, and high. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

Reasoning is a real but minor lever, and its sign differs between the two LLMs tested. Both shifts are roughly 9 percentage points and small compared to the cross-provider gap reported in §5.1.1. The result complicates any general claim that more reasoning makes a generative agent uniformly more or less cautious.

5.1.3 Model size (RQ3) — Does LLM size affect mobility curves?

Model size refers to a provider’s relative scale tier within a single family of releases (see §3.2). Two providers in the data have multiple model sizes within a single generation: Anthropic’s Claude 4.5 family (Haiku [small], Sonnet [middle], Opus [large]) and Google’s Gemini 2.5 family (Flash Lite [small], Flash [middle]). All five configurations are evaluated with reasoning off. Figure 6 places the two size ladders side by side.

For the Anthropic Claude 4.5 family, mobility does not change monotonically with size. Going from Haiku (small) to Sonnet (middle) shifts the curve substantially downward — Sonnet sits well below Haiku across the full infection range. But going from Sonnet to Opus (large) reverses direction: Opus tracks close to Haiku rather than continuing further down. For the Google Gemini 2.5 family, Gemini 2.5 Flash sits well above Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite across most of the infection range. Flash Lite itself shows an unusual within-curve pattern — its mobility starts moderate at 0% infection, dips between roughly 0% and 1%, then recovers above 50% from 1.5% onward. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

Across both providers’ size ladders, the data shows substantial variation but no consistent direction of effect attributable to size.

5.1.4 Model evolution (RQ4) — Does LLM evolution affect mobility curves?

Model evolution refers to the sequence of major releases within a single provider’s family over time, where each successive release typically incorporates new training data, refined alignment, and architectural improvements (see §3.2). All three providers contribute model evolution data: Anthropic from Claude Sonnet 4.0 to Claude Sonnet 4.5; OpenAI from GPT-3.5 Turbo through GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, and GPT-5.2; and Google from Gemini 2.0 Flash through Gemini 2.5 Flash to Gemini 3 Flash Preview. All configurations are evaluated with reasoning off. Figure 7 places the three evolution sequences side by side.

The Anthropic sequence has only two points: Claude Sonnet 4.5 sits modestly above Claude Sonnet 4.0 across the infection range. The OpenAI sequence shows wide swings between successive releases — GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-5.2 sit near the cautious end of the mobility range; GPT-4o sits at the mobile end; GPT-4.1 and GPT-5.1 sit at intermediate positions. Going from one release to the next does not move the OpenAI curve in a consistent direction. The Google sequence shows a large upward shift from Gemini 2.0 Flash to Gemini 2.5 Flash, then almost no further change between Gemini 2.5 Flash and Gemini 3 Flash Preview. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

Across the three providers, model evolution does not produce a consistent direction of effect on mobility, and the within-provider patterns differ from each other.

5.1.5 Knowledge cutoff (RQ5) — Does LLM knowledge cutoff affect mobility curves?

Knowledge cutoff is the date through which the model’s training data extends; models with later cutoffs have been exposed to more recent text from the public internet (see §3.2). The 21 configurations span knowledge cutoffs from September 2021 (GPT-3.5 Turbo) to August 2025 (Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.2). Figure 8 plots each configuration on a knowledge-cutoff timeline, showing the four era groupings used in the analysis below: pre-2024, mid-2024 (Jun–Sep), early 2025 (Jan–Mar), and late 2025 (Aug). Figure 9 then shows the mobility curves within each era.

Within each era panel, the curves do not cluster by knowledge cutoff. Models with the same or near-identical training-data cutoff date land at very different positions on the mobility axis: in the pre-2024 panel, GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4o sit at opposite ends of the mobility range despite cutoffs only two years apart; in the mid-2024 panel, four configurations with cutoffs in a 3-month window (Jun–Sep 2024) span nearly the full mobility range; in the early-2025 panel, six configurations with cutoffs within a 3-month window again span the full range; in the late-2025 panel, Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.2 share an August 2025 cutoff but produce distinctly different curves. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

Knowledge cutoff does not predict mobility curve placement in this data. Whatever drives the cross-LLM variation reported in §5.1.1, it is not the recency of training data alone.

5.1.6 Release date (RQ6) — Does LLM release date affect mobility curves?

Release date is the date the model was made publicly available via API; it is generally independent of the model’s knowledge cutoff (see §3.2). The 21 configurations span release dates from January 2024 (GPT-3.5 Turbo) to December 2025 (GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview). Figure 10 plots each configuration on a release-date timeline, showing the three era groupings used in the analysis below: legacy (pre-2025), early 2025 (Feb–Jun), and late 2025 (Sep–Dec). Figure 11 then shows the mobility curves within each era.

As with knowledge cutoff, configurations sharing a release-date era do not cluster on the mobility axis. The legacy panel shows GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4o at opposite ends of the range despite release dates within a year of each other. The early-2025 panel shows six configurations released within a five-month window spanning the full mobility range. The late-2025 panel shows six configurations released within a four-month window also spanning a wide range. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

Release date does not predict mobility curve placement in this data, and does not explain the cross-LLM variation observed in §5.1.1.

5.2 Persona Results

The four subsections below examine the agent-level predictors of the stay-home decision and how the 21 LLMs vary in their treatment of those predictors. §5.2.1 examines the direction of each persona predictor’s effect (RQ7); §5.2.2 ranks persona predictors by their relative importance within and across LLMs (RQ8); §5.2.3 the magnitude of all persona effects combined relative to the infection-level effect (RQ9); and §5.2.4 cross-LLM agreement on which agent personas are most cautious (RQ10).

5.2.1 Trait effect directions (RQ7) — What is the direction of each persona predictor’s effect on the stay-home decision?

Agent traits enter the random-effects logit (§4.7.2) as predictors: five Big Five trait dummies, a male dummy, and age in years. Each Big Five dummy equals 1 for the named pole (extraverted, agreeable, conscientious, emotionally stable, open to experience) and 0 for its opposite (introverted, antagonistic, unconscientious, neurotic, closed to experience). The male dummy is 1 for male and 0 for female. Age is in raw years; its coefficient is per year, and is scaled by 47 (the 18–65 span) for magnitude comparisons against the binary traits. Each predictor’s β gives the log-odds shift in stay-home probability associated with the dummy = 1 value, holding the other predictors fixed. A negative β on a trait dummy means agents at that pole are more mobile, and a positive β means they stay home more.

Figure 12 shows the per-predictor β coefficients across all 21 LLM configurations, one panel per predictor, with significance markers and 95% confidence intervals. The right column of each panel reports a normalized |β| / infection-range ratio, where infection range is the maximum log-odds shift the infection signal produces over the 0–7% range.

For four of the five Big Five dimensions, every LLM agrees on the sign of the coefficient. Extraversion is negative in all 21 configurations (significant in 20). Agreeableness is positive in all 21 (significant in 18). Conscientiousness is positive in all 21 (significant in 19). Emotional stability is negative in 20 of 21 (significant in 19). The unanimity holds within each provider: all 5 Anthropic, all 9 OpenAI, and all 7 Gemini configurations sign these four traits in the same direction.

Openness to experience is the least consistent Big Five dimension: 14 of 21 configurations show a significant positive effect, and only one (Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite) shows a significant negative effect. Gender (male) is significant in only 3 of 21 configurations and varies in direction across configurations. Age (scaled across the 18–65 range) is significant in 6 of 21 configurations, mostly within OpenAI.

5.2.2 Predictor importance (RQ8) — Which persona predictors most strongly drive the stay-home decision?

To rank persona predictors by influence within and across LLMs, each predictor’s |β| is divided by that LLM’s infection range (§5.2.1). A ratio above 1× means the predictor alone shifts the decision more than infection’s full effect. Figure 13 plots each configuration’s predictor ratios on one row, with a vertical 1× line marking the threshold.

Gemini configurations sit furthest right. Four of the five Big Five traits have median ratios above 1× (extraversion 1.83, conscientiousness 1.40, emotional stability 1.35, openness 1.09; only agreeableness sits just below at 0.93), meaning each of those four traits typically exceeds infection’s full effect on its own. Anthropic configurations cluster lower, with median ratios spanning a wide range (conscientiousness 0.75, extraversion 0.64, emotional stability 0.61, agreeableness 0.27, openness 0.04); across all five configurations and five Big Five traits, only one ratio crosses 1× (Claude Opus 4.5’s conscientiousness, at 1.09×). OpenAI configurations cluster lowest, with all five Big Five median ratios below 0.4× (extraversion 0.35, emotional stability 0.24, conscientiousness 0.19, agreeableness 0.17, openness 0.15); no OpenAI trait coefficient anywhere in the figure exceeds 1×, indicating that for OpenAI configurations trait identity is a smaller share of the decision than that of infection level. Across all three providers, gender and age (diamonds) sit near zero on the ratio axis, consistent with their low significance counts in §5.2.1.

The rank-1 predictor (the predictor with the largest |β| / infection ratio) within each configuration also splits along provider lines. In all 9 OpenAI configurations, infection level is the rank-1 predictor. In 4 of 5 Anthropic configurations, infection is rank-1; only Claude Opus 4.5 promotes a Big Five trait (conscientiousness) to rank-1. In 6 of 7 Gemini configurations a Big Five trait is rank-1 (extraversion in 4 configurations, emotional stability in 2); only Gemini 2.0 Flash keeps infection at rank-1. The disagreement on which predictor matters most is itself a provider-level pattern: OpenAI is uniformly infection-dominated, Gemini is uniformly trait-dominated, and Anthropic is mostly infection-dominated with a single Opus exception.

5.2.3 Persona vs. infection magnitude (RQ9) — Are personality effects larger than infection effects?

The persona-to-infection ratio compares total persona influence against the infection effect. The denominator is the same infection range used in §5.2.1 and §5.2.2; the numerator, the personality range, is the maximum gap in stay-home log-odds achievable by varying an agent’s persona (Big Five traits, gender, age) at zero infection — a sum of |β| across all persona predictors from the random-effects logit. A ratio above 1× means persona variation alone shifts the decision more than infection’s full effect. Figure 14 plots both ranges on a common log-odds scale: the solid line spans the personality range at zero infection, and the dashed line shifts the same range by the maximum infection contribution.

Across the 21 configurations, 19 have a persona-to-infection ratio above 1× and the median ratio is 2.75×. The provider-level pattern parallels the per-trait pattern in §5.2.1. Gemini configurations are the most persona-dominated, with a median ratio of 7.96×: five of the seven Gemini configurations (Gemini 2.5 Flash plus all four Gemini 3 Flash Preview reasoning levels) reach ratios between 7.07× and 8.85×, while the older Gemini 2.0 Flash and Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite sit lower at 2.93× and 2.75×. Anthropic configurations are intermediate, with a median of 2.84× and a spread from 1.17× (Claude 3 Haiku) to 3.67× (Claude Opus 4.5). OpenAI configurations are the most infection-dominated, with a median of 1.37× and most configurations between 1× and 2×; GPT-4.1 (3.09×) sits noticeably above the OpenAI median, and two configurations sit below 1× (GPT-5.2 off at 0.08×, GPT-5.2 low at 0.81×).

Beyond the persona-to-infection ratio, Figure 14 also shows where each LLM’s personality range sits on the absolute log-odds scale at 0% infection. For 19 of the 21 configurations, the personality range crosses the 50% P(stay-home) threshold: the most-cautious persona sits above 50% and the most-mobile persona sits below, with infection then shifting the range further toward stay-home. Two configurations are exceptions, both with personality ranges entirely below 50%: Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-4o. For these two LLMs, every persona is inclined to go out at 0% infection regardless of trait combination. No configuration has a personality range sitting entirely above 50% — at zero infection, every LLM produces at least one persona that would go out.

5.2.4 Agent identity agreement (RQ10) — Do LLMs agree on which agent personas are most cautious?

For each LLM configuration, the 100 agents are independently ranked by their stay-home rate within that configuration — the fraction of the agent’s 200 decisions (40 infection levels × 5 reps) on which the agent chose to stay home. Rank 1 is the most cautious persona under that LLM. Ties are resolved by average-rank tie handling. Each agent then has 21 ranks, one for each LLM configuration. Figure 15 summarizes each agent’s 21 ranks: the dot is the median rank the agent received, the dark bar spans the IQR (25th–75th percentile), and the pale whisker spans the full range. Agents are sorted top-to-bottom by median rank.

Cross-LLM agreement is uneven across rank positions. The median IQR among the top-25 most-cautious agents is 16 ranks; among the bottom-25 most-mobile agents, 14 ranks; but among the middle 50 agents it widens to 26 ranks. LLMs converge more readily on agents at the extremes than on agents in the middle of the cautious-to-mobile distribution. The median full range echoes this pattern — 48 ranks among the top 25, 62 among the bottom 25, and 68 among the middle 50. No agent escapes substantial outlier disagreement: the smallest range across the 21 LLMs is 27 ranks, and the median agent has a 66-rank range.

5.3 Response Results

The three subsections below examine the content of the reasoning passage. §5.3.1 (RQ11) examines how trait mentions shape the stay-home decision; §5.3.2 (RQ12) which concept categories are cited in stay-home versus go-out reasoning; and §5.3.3 (RQ13) whether the reasoning is genuinely persona-specific or templated.

5.3.1 Trait mentions and behavioral effect (RQ11) — How do trait mentions shape the decision?

The mention-flag logit (§4.7.3) tests whether mentioning a Big Five trait label, infection, or age in the agent’s reasoning text amplifies or dampens the behavioral effect already attributable to the agent’s actual trait, gender, age, and infection level. Each mention coefficient γk is the log-odds shift in stay-home probability when concept k is mentioned, holding the rest of the model fixed. A flag enters the model only when its overall mention rate falls in [15%, 85%]; outside that window it carries no usable variation. Figure 16 reports the per-LLM mention rates, broken out by the agent’s assigned Big Five trait label. For example, the cell at (Claude Opus 4.5, Introverted) shows the share of Opus 4.5’s introverted agents whose reasoning mentioned an extraversion keyword — either an “introvert”/“introverted” word (e.g., “I am introverted”) or an “extravert”/“extraverted” word (e.g., “I am not extraverted”) counts. Infection and age each have a single column covering all responses. Figure 17 reports the mention coefficients for the flags whose mention rate passes the [15%, 85%] gate.

Mention rates vary widely both within and across providers (Figure 16). Infection level is mentioned in nearly every response on most LLMs (mean rate 87.8% across the 21 configurations) and falls outside the 15–85% gate for most of them, so the infection mention flag enters the regression for only 4 of 21 configurations. Among the Big Five labels, conscientiousness is mentioned most often (mean 55.8%) and openness least (mean 16.6%). Provider-level differences are substantial: average Big Five mention rate is 57.7% across the five Anthropic configurations, 17.1% across the nine OpenAI configurations, and 48.7% across the seven Gemini configurations. GPT-3.5 Turbo is an outlier on every dimension (it mentions infection in only 0.2% of responses and Big Five labels in under 2%), reasoning that is largely silent on the very inputs the prompt provides. Age is rarely cited anywhere (mean 4.0% across configurations).

For four of the five reliably-signed Big Five labels, the mention-flag effect aligns with the trait’s behavioral direction (Figure 17). Mentioning “introverted” shifts the stay-home log-odds positive in 9 of 12 configurations where the flag was estimable; mentioning “extraverted” shifts negative in 9 of 11; mentioning “neurotic” shifts positive in 12 of 12; mentioning “emotionally stable” shifts negative in 12 of 12; mentioning “antagonistic” shifts negative in 10 of 11. In each case the mention amplifies the trait’s behavioral pull beyond what the trait dummy alone captures.

Conscientiousness is a paradoxical exception. Although conscientious agents stay home more (§5.2.1), mentioning “conscientious” shifts the log-odds toward going out in 9 of 11 configurations, with no configuration showing a significant positive shift. Mentioning “unconscientious” also predominantly shifts toward going out (8 of 13). One plausible reading is that conscientiousness is invoked in counterfactual or instrumental framings (“I’m being conscientious by going out to handle responsibilities”) rather than as a direct stay-home marker. Openness shows weaker, mixed mention patterns, consistent with the weaker openness trait effects in §5.2.1.

Where the infection mention flag enters the model (4 of 21 configurations; the rest fall outside the 15–85% gate because nearly every response mentions infection), the coefficient is positive and significant in all 4: when the agent’s reasoning explicitly mentions infection, the stay-home decision is more likely beyond what the actual infection level alone would predict. The age mention flag enters only one configuration and is not significant.

5.3.2 Decision themes (RQ12) — Which concept categories are cited in stay-home vs. go-out reasoning?

Agents’ reasoning text invokes broader concept categories beyond individual Big Five labels. A concept dictionary (§4.7.3) tags each response for mentions of Work, Community, Virus Properties, Self Health, and Virus Prevalence. The stay-vs-go comparison — the difference between the mention rate within stay-home responses and within go-out responses, in percentage points — reveals which concepts each LLM uses to rationalize each direction. Figure 18 reports this comparison for the ten Big Five trait poles plus infection and age (the Δ view of Figure 16); Figure 19 reports it for the five concept categories.

At the pole level, agents asymmetrically invoke trait labels that align with their decision direction. Across the 21 configurations, “neurotic” is cited a median 20 percentage points more often in stay-home responses than in go-out (a cautious-coded pole, per §5.2.1); “introverted” 16 pp more. Conversely, “emotionally stable” is cited 13 pp more in go-out reasoning, and “extraverted” 10 pp more (both mobile-coded poles). The asymmetry is direction-aligned with the trait-effect signs from §5.2.1 and complements the mention-flag amplification finding from §5.3.1: agents both invoke the matching label more often and, conditional on invoking it, get nudged further in that direction.

Concept categories show much sharper stay-vs-go asymmetries than trait labels (Figure 19). Community concerns (“others,” “family,” “community,” “spread”) are cited a median 44 pp more often in stay-home reasoning than in go-out reasoning, with provider medians of +50 pp (Anthropic), +50 pp (OpenAI), and +30 pp (Gemini). Virus Properties (descriptions of how the virus behaves) are cited 20 pp more in stay-home reasoning, with the largest skew in Gemini (+53 pp). On the go-out side, Self Health (“I feel fine,” “healthy,” “asymptomatic”) is cited a median 37 pp more often in go-out responses, reaching −56 pp in OpenAI; Work obligations are cited 8 pp more in go-out reasoning. Virus Prevalence is roughly symmetric.

Stay-home reasoning skews toward other-focused concepts (Community, Virus Properties); go-out reasoning skews toward self-focused concepts (Self Health, Work). The asymmetry holds across all three providers, though magnitudes differ — OpenAI shows the strongest Self-Health skew on the go-out side, while Gemini shows the strongest Virus-Properties skew on the stay-home side.

5.3.3 Persona individuation (RQ13) — Does the LLM produce genuinely persona-specific reasoning, or use the same template regardless of who the agent is?

The two preceding subsections describe what concepts agents’ responses cite. Persona individuation asks a deeper question: at the level of overall semantic content, does an LLM produce different reasoning for different personas, or does it template? For each (LLM, infection level), the analysis restricts to agents whose 5 repetitions all returned the same decision (the “5/5 agreement” subset). Within-agent text similarity is the mean cosine similarity of OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large vectors across an agent’s 5 reps. Across-agent text similarity is the mean cosine across all pairs of agents that unanimously chose the same direction at the same infection level. The gap Δ = (within − across) measures persona individuation: a large positive gap means the LLM produces text more similar within an agent than across agents (persona-specific reasoning); a near-zero gap means the LLM produces similar text regardless of who the agent is (templated reasoning).

Cosine similarity values can be hard to interpret in isolation. Figure 20 anchors the scale with three example response pairs from one LLM (Claude Opus 4.5) at different cosine values, ranging from a near-paraphrase pair (within-agent, ≈0.99) to a substantively different pair (across-agent, opposite decisions, ≈0.65). Figure 21 then reports the within−across gap per LLM.

All 21 LLMs have a positive within−across gap, meaning every LLM produces at least somewhat persona-specific reasoning. Magnitudes vary considerably. Anthropic configurations have a median Δ of 0.118 (range 0.082 to 0.164). OpenAI configurations sit lowest with a median Δ of 0.035 (range 0.012 to 0.163). Gemini configurations show the largest gap with a median Δ of 0.131 (range 0.109 to 0.241; the maximum is in Gemini 2.5 Flash). Within OpenAI, the GPT-5.2 reasoning ladder produces the smallest gaps (Δ between 0.012 and 0.026 across off/low/medium/high) and o3 is also small at 0.035; reasoning-enabled OpenAI configurations produce notably more templated text than reasoning-off OpenAI configurations like GPT-4o (Δ = 0.163) or GPT-4.1 (Δ = 0.145). The provider ordering on persona individuation (Gemini » Anthropic » OpenAI) parallels the persona-effect-magnitude ordering from §5.2.2 and §5.2.3: LLMs that weight persona inputs more heavily in their decisions also produce more persona-specific reasoning text.

6. Discussion

The Discussion synthesizes the §5 results around the research questions. §6.1 covers RQ1–RQ6 (the mobility-curve lenses). §6.2 covers RQ7 and RQ8 (trait effect directions and predictor importance). §6.3 covers RQ9 (persona-vs-infection balance). §6.4 covers RQ10 (agent identity agreement). §6.5 draws practical implications for researchers building generative-agent simulations on LLM substrates. Discussion of RQ11–RQ13 is deferred until the corresponding response-results subsections are drafted.

6.1 Cross-LLM variation: what predicts it and what does not

The 21 mobility curves span nearly the full 0–100% stay-home range, but the six lenses examined in §5.1 do not contribute equally to that variation. Provider (RQ1) is the most useful organizing axis. Each provider’s portfolio occupies a distinct band of the mobility space, and matched flagships at reasoning off — GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5, Gemini 3 Flash Preview — occupy clearly distinct regions: GPT-5.2 collapses to near-zero mobility almost immediately, while Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview remain in the 75–90% mobility band across the infection range and are statistically indistinguishable from each other (§5.1.1). Provider therefore constitutes the largest coherent driver of the cross-LLM variation.

The remaining within-family lenses do not produce a consistent directional effect. Reasoning level (RQ2) shifts mobility by roughly 9 percentage points in either direction, with opposite signs in the two configurations sampled across the full ladder — GPT-5.2 becomes more mobile as reasoning increases, Gemini 3 Flash Preview becomes less mobile (§5.1.2). Model size within a family (RQ3) is non-monotonic in Anthropic’s Claude 4.5 family (Sonnet sits below both Haiku and Opus) and shows a wide spread but no general direction in Google’s Gemini 2.5 family (§5.1.3). Model evolution within a provider (RQ4) is direction-inconsistent: OpenAI’s release sequence swings from highly cautious (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-5.2) to highly mobile (GPT-4o) and back without monotonic progression; Google shows a single large step (Gemini 2.0 → 2.5) followed by a plateau; Anthropic’s two-point sequence shows a small upward shift (§5.1.4).

Knowledge cutoff (RQ5) and release date (RQ6) fail to predict mobility-curve placement at all. Configurations with cutoffs in a three-month window span nearly the full mobility range, and configurations released in a four-month window do likewise (§5.1.5, §5.1.6). Whatever produces the cross-LLM variation in mobility behavior, it is not training-data recency, release recency, model size, generational position, or reasoning level. The variation is large but is keyed to the LLM lineage as a whole rather than to any of the lenses that practitioners typically use to choose between models. Identifying what within a provider’s training pipeline produces a given mobility tendency — alignment goals, RLHF rater pool, system-prompt practices, decoding defaults — is left as future work.

6.2 Trait directions and predictor importance (RQ7, RQ8)

The persona analysis (§5.2.1, §5.2.2) shows two complementary patterns: directions agree across LLMs, importance does not. The direction of trait effects (RQ7) is reliably signed across all 21 configurations: 21 of 21 agree that extraverted agents are more mobile and that agreeable, conscientious agents stay home more, and 20 of 21 agree that emotionally stable agents are more mobile. Openness is mostly positive, gender and age are weak predictors that rarely reach significance, and the unanimity on the four reliably signed traits holds within each provider as well as across providers. This is consistent with a shared cultural model of how Big Five labels map onto risk-related behavior, plausibly encoded by alignment training on broadly similar corpora, and is reassuring for GABM realism: a researcher can expect that swapping the LLM does not change which traits make a generative agent more cautious.

The importance of each predictor (RQ8), in contrast, splits sharply along provider lines. In all 9 OpenAI configurations the rank-1 predictor is infection level; in 6 of 7 Gemini configurations the rank-1 predictor is a Big Five trait; in Anthropic infection is rank-1 in 4 of 5 with Claude Opus 4.5 the only exception. The same provider ordering shows up in median |β| / infection ratios for the Big Five (Gemini » Anthropic » OpenAI): four of five Gemini Big Five median ratios exceed 1×, only one Anthropic ratio crosses 1× (Opus 4.5’s conscientiousness), and no OpenAI ratio reaches 1×. A simulation built on a Gemini backbone will therefore exhibit more persona-driven heterogeneity in agent behavior than the same simulation built on an OpenAI backbone, even when the agent population, prompt, and infection signal are identical. The 21 LLMs broadly agree on which way each trait pushes the decision but disagree on how much weight each predictor carries, and that disagreement is itself a provider-level pattern.

6.3 The persona-vs-infection balance (RQ9)

For most LLMs in this study, who an agent is matters more than what the perceived infection level is. 19 of 21 configurations show a personality range exceeding the 0–7% infection range, with a median ratio of 2.75×. The pattern is not universal — the two GPT-5.2 settings at off and low reasoning are infection-dominated — but the typical generative agent here is shaped more by its persona than by the community-infection signal. The provider-level spread (Gemini median 7.96×, Anthropic 2.84×, OpenAI 1.37×) means the relative weight of persona versus environment in driving agent decisions differs roughly six-fold across providers: on a Gemini backbone, agent behavior will be dominated by persona; on an OpenAI backbone, persona and environmental signal will be near-balanced. The two infection-dominated GPT-5.2 configurations are also configurations where mobility collapses to near zero across the full infection range (§5.1.1), suggesting that when an LLM is maximally cautious to the infection signal, it crowds the persona signal out as a side effect.

6.4 Agent rank consensus (RQ10)

Cross-LLM agreement on agent identity is uneven across the cautious-to-mobile distribution. At the extremes, LLMs converge sharply: the median IQR among the 25 most-cautious agents is 16 ranks, and among the 25 most-mobile is 14 ranks. In the middle of the distribution, the IQR widens to 26 ranks. This pattern is consistent with the §6.2 finding that LLMs agree on the direction of trait effects but disagree on their magnitudes. Agents whose persona profiles stack many cautious-coded poles (or many mobile-coded poles) get ranked similarly on every LLM because the direction of each trait’s effect is universal; agents with mixed trait profiles are sensitive to which traits a given LLM weighs more heavily, and that weighting is itself a provider-level pattern.

For GABM design, the 100 frozen agents do behave as a recognizable population at the population level: the median rank ordering is broadly preserved across all 21 LLMs. But no agent escapes substantial outlier disagreement — even the most-agreed-upon agent has a 27-rank gap between its highest and lowest LLM ranks, and the median agent has a 66-rank range. A simulation that selects agents by their position at the cautious or mobile extreme (e.g., the 10 most-cautious agents) can transfer that selection across LLMs with reasonable fidelity. A simulation that depends on the precise rank of a middle-of-the-pack agent on a specific LLM should not assume that ranking will hold on a different LLM.

6.5 Implications for GABM design

Three practical implications follow for researchers using LLMs as the cognitive substrate of generative agents.

The choice of LLM is not interchangeable. Aggregate mobility, the magnitude of persona effects, and the relative weight of persona versus the infection signal all depend on the LLM. There is no shortcut for estimating from the outside what an arbitrary LLM will produce in a generative-agent setting; the only currently reliable approach is to probe the LLM under the conditions of interest, as done in this study.

Within-family upgrades do not move agent behavior in a predictable direction. The lenses practitioners typically use to choose between models — bigger model, newer release, more recent training data, more reasoning — do not, in this domain, predict generative-agent behavior. A researcher who upgrades from one model to its successor, scales to a larger model in the same family, or enables reasoning should not assume agent behavior will change in a known direction; in several cases observed here, the change reverses sign across providers or across releases.

Persona inputs generalize directionally but not in magnitude. Assigning Big Five trait labels to agents will reliably push behavior in expected directions on every LLM tested. But a researcher building a population-heterogeneity argument — for example, claiming that a simulation reproduces the spread of behaviors observed in a real population — should report the persona-effect magnitude on the LLM used, because that magnitude varies several-fold across providers and shapes how much of the simulation’s agent-to-agent variation is doing real work.
