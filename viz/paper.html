LLM Sensitivity of Generative Agents: Evidence from an Epidemic Model

Ross Williams
Industrial and Systems Engineering, Virginia Tech; Falls Church, VA 22043, USA.
Email: rossfw@vt.edu

Abstract

Generative agent-based modeling (GABM) replaces the rule-based decision-making of classical agent-based models with LLM-mediated decisions, enabling researchers to simulate human behavior that resists mathematical specification. The original epidemic GABM and its follow-on prompt-sensitivity study both used GPT-3.5, leaving the generalizability of GABM findings to other LLMs untested. We address this with a frozen-task probe study: 100 personas, 40 community-infection levels, and 5 repetitions across 21 LLM configurations from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, totaling 420,000 binary stay-home decisions during a simulated epidemic. Most LLMs agree on the direction of persona effects, but magnitudes vary several-fold by provider: largest in Gemini agents, intermediate in Claude, smallest in GPT. The model attributes a researcher would typically use to choose an LLM (size, generation, knowledge cutoff, release date) give no insight into where any given configuration’s behavior lands. The reasoning text accompanying each decision encodes substantive predictive signal beyond the decision itself, and reveals an other-focused-vs-self-focused framing of the stay-home choice. We argue that LLM choice is itself a parameterization of agent behavior: GABM with general-purpose LLMs is most defensible for qualitative claims about agent behavior and weakest for the quantitative claims modelers most often want to make. LLM sensitivity should become a routine evidentiary expectation in GABM publications.

1. Introduction

Generative agent-based modeling (GABM) was introduced as a paradigm for incorporating human behavior into simulations of complex social systems ghaffarzadegan2024. Where a traditional agent-based model relies on the modeler to write decision rules and tune their parameters, a GABM connects each agent to a large language model (LLM) and lets the LLM decide the agent’s actions. This sidesteps a long-standing barrier: human behavior in many domains resists clean mathematical specification, and a modeler’s hand-coded rules typically capture only a thin slice of what real people consider when making a decision.

In the two years since GABM was introduced, the LLM landscape has expanded dramatically: dozens of models span three major providers, multiple generations within each provider, distinct training-data eras, and configurable parameters such as reasoning level. As these models have scaled, they have acquired emergent reasoning and behavioral capabilities that smaller predecessors lacked — generalizing arithmetic wei_emergent2022, performing multi-step inference wei_cot2022, and learning new tasks from in-context examples brown2020 — only above certain training thresholds. As the LLMs that drive generative agents have evolved across this period, have they converged on consistent simulated behavior, or does the LLM choice itself remain a parameter of any GABM result?

The answer determines what GABM as a method can credibly claim. If LLM choice does not materially shape simulated behavior, LLMs are interchangeable and GABM is a robust technique that can be applied regardless of the model a researcher selects. If LLM choice does shape simulated behavior, every published GABM result is implicitly conditional on the LLM that produced it — complicating the method’s foundational claim that LLM decision-making removes the explicit behavioral parameterization characteristic of classical agent-based modeling epstein2007.

Two recent peer-reviewed reviews of GABM have raised convergent concerns about the field’s credibility. Larooij and Törnberg larooij_tornberg2026 argue that the integration of LLMs may exacerbate rather than resolve ABM’s long-standing challenges of empirical validation, given the black-box structure, stochastic outputs, and cultural biases of language models. Li and Wu liwu2025 argue that prompt-engineering choices in current GABM studies over-control simulation outcomes to the point that emergent behavior often reflects researcher design rather than agent dynamics. Both reviews flag the LLM as a source of methodological concern, but neither systematically measures how LLM choice propagates into simulated agent behavior. Recent surveys of the GABM literature wang2023, mou2024, gao2024hss catalog the proliferation of applications while treating the LLM as a black-box backend whose specific identity often does not enter the analytical frame. A growing body of work probes LLM-driven agents across many models in survey dominguez_olmedo2024, mooney2025, game-theoretic einwiller2025, and forecasting parikh2026 settings, but none has been conducted inside a GABM-style decision loop.

To address this empirical gap, we revisit the GABM epidemic model of Williams et al. williams2023 as our probe domain. The setting is well-suited to systematic probing across LLMs: the agent decision is binary (stay home or go out), allowing clean statistical comparison across models; each decision is accompanied by a free-text reasoning passage, enabling content analysis alongside quantitative measurement; agent personas vary along the Big Five trait dimensions, supporting persona-level regression at the individual-decision level; and the model is itself a published GABM whose sensitivity to LLM choice is precisely the kind of question this study seeks to answer.

Two prior studies have used this model. Williams et al. williams2023 introduced it on GPT-3.5, demonstrating that generative agents could produce realistic individual decisions and emergent population-level dynamics, including multiple infection waves and an endemic period. Williams williams_prompt2023 then characterized the model’s sensitivity to prompt design — how different expressions, names, and situations shape agent behavior. The present study characterizes its sensitivity to the LLM itself.

The probe design holds the same 100 personas (name, age, and Big Five trait combination) and 40 community-infection levels (0% to 7%) constant across 21 LLM configurations from three providers, prompting each agent at each infection level 5 times to account for LLM stochasticity, yielding 420,000 binary decisions. The 21 configurations span six lenses for comparison across LLMs: provider, reasoning level, model size within a family, model evolution across releases, knowledge cutoff, and release date. Each response includes a free-text reasoning passage alongside the binary decision, allowing both quantitative comparison of decision distributions and content analysis of how the agents reason. We analyze the responses through three progressively deeper analyses: aggregate mobility curves at the configuration level, persona-level random-effects logistic regressions that test how each persona attribute shapes the binary decision, and content analysis of the reasoning passages.

The investigation is organized around fourteen research questions, grouped by the three analyses. Table 1 lists each question alongside the section in which its results are presented and the figures that bear on it.

Across the 21 configurations, the results indicate most LLMs agree on the direction of persona effects but disagree several-fold on magnitude. None of the model attributes of an LLM — size, generation, knowledge cutoff, release date — give any insight into where the configuration’s behavior lands. The reasoning text encodes substantive predictive signal beyond the decision itself. We argue these findings reframe what GABM with general-purpose LLMs can credibly claim: the method is well-suited to qualitative claims about agent behavior, but the LLM choice itself functions as a parameterization for any quantitative claim. Over this two-year LLM evolution arc, generative-agent behavior has not converged.

2. Background & Related Work

2.1 Generative agent-based modeling

In a generative agent-based modeling (GABM) simulation, agents’ decisions are produced by an LLM rather than by hand-coded behavioral rules ghaffarzadegan2024. At every step the LLM is prompted with the agent’s persona and the current state of the simulation. Then, the LLM’s response is parsed into a structured action that updates that state for the next step. Ghaffarzadegan et al. formalized this loop as a methodology for combining LLM-driven decisions with classical agent-based modeling ghaffarzadegan2024.

Existing GABMs span a range of domains. Williams et al. modeled an epidemic in which agents decide each day whether to stay home or go out williams2023, a study cited as a canonical GABM example in subsequent reviews lu_aleta_moreno2024, adornetto2025. Wu et al. introduce Smart Agent-Based Modeling, a general GABM framework demonstrated across economics, finance, and engineering case studies wu_sabm2023. Vezhnevets et al. propose Concordia, a library for grounding GABM actions in physical, social, and digital spaces using a game-master that mediates state transitions vezhnevets_concordia2023. Lan et al. deploy LLM agents as players in the social-deduction game Avalon, observing emergent collaboration, confrontation, leadership, persuasion, and camouflage behaviors across multi-round gameplay lan_avalon2024. Across these systems the common pattern is the same: many agents, many timesteps, environment-coupled decisions, with collective dynamics emerging from individual LLM-driven choices.

2.2 LLM social simulation

A broader literature uses LLMs as social-science instruments without embedding them inside a multi-step GABM loop. Park et al.’s generative-agents architecture introduced the memory-stream, reflection, and planning pattern in a virtual-town sandbox of 25 agents pursuing daily goals and exchanging messages park2023. Gao et al.’s S3 social-network simulator deploys LLM agents that observe content, adjust attitudes, and produce posts, reproducing empirical patterns of emotion and attitude propagation around two news events gao_s3_2023. Akata et al. have LLM agents play repeated games against one another to study cooperation akata2023. Argyle et al. introduced “algorithmic fidelity” as the property by which prompted LLMs reproduce demographic-conditional opinion distributions argyle2023. Horton showed that LLM agents exhibit behaviors consistent with classical economic-agent assumptions horton2023. Binz and Schulz applied cognitive-psychology paradigms to GPT-3 and found human-like response patterns alongside human-like biases binz_schulz2023. These studies provide individual-level behavioral evidence for the use of LLMs in social-science settings.

A separate line of recent work develops methods for raising the fidelity with which an LLM reproduces a specific human or human population. Park et al.’s 1,000-personas study prompts agents with detailed interview transcripts from real participants to align each agent with its human counterpart park2024_1000. Kolluri et al. fine-tune LLMs directly on human-behavior traces from social-science experiments kolluri2025. Efforts such as Simile.ai go further still, training purpose-built models for population-level diversity rather than the single polished persona of a chat assistant simile_ai. The present study uses general-purpose chat models with light persona prompts; whether richer prompting, fine-tuning, or purpose-built models reduce the model-to-model variability documented here is left as future work.

2.3 LLM evaluations

LLMs are routinely evaluated on standardized benchmarks of capability — multi-domain knowledge (MMLU mmlu_hendrycks2021), program synthesis (HumanEval humaneval_chen2021), broad task suites (BIG-Bench bigbench_srivastava2022) — and on large-N head-to-head rankings via crowdsourced human preference (Chatbot Arena chatbot_arena_chiang2024). These evaluations measure what an LLM can do in isolation. They have established that frontier models differ measurably from earlier or smaller predecessors along most capability dimensions, with several capabilities emerging only above particular scale thresholds wei_emergent2022.

A second strand of work uses shallow behavioral probes — short, persona-conditioned tasks — to test how the LLM affects an agent’s behavior. Dominguez-Olmedo et al. dominguez_olmedo2024 administer multiple-choice survey instruments to 43 LLMs and report that apparent cross-model differences in opinion distributions are largely artifacts of response-ordering bias; once corrected, models collapse toward near-uniform random responding regardless of size or training corpus. Einwiller et al. einwiller2025 run a matched dictator-game probe across multiple LLMs with systematic prompt variations and find substantial cross-model divergence in giving rates and prompt sensitivity. Mooney et al. mooney2025 generate roughly five thousand demographic agents and test behavioral coherence across nine models, finding that while surface-level survey-style responses align broadly with each agent’s stated profile, behavior in subsequent agent-to-agent interactions departs from that profile, and both model family and model size shape how often the coherence breaks down. Parikh et al. parikh2026 compare nine LLM configurations against polling data from the 2024 U.S. presidential election and report systematic, model-specific divergence in favorability predictions. These studies establish that LLM choice can move behavioral output measurably.

The present study is another such probe, applied specifically to the persona-conditioned decision that feeds an epidemic GABM. The same 100 personas are presented at 40 infection levels to 21 LLM configurations, holding the prompt, persona, and scenario fixed, varying only the underlying model. Where capability benchmarks ask what an LLM can do and behavioral-fidelity experiments ask whether it acts like a human, the analysis here asks how much the LLM shapes the agent’s stay-or-go decision at the moment a GABM would query its agent. LLMs that perform similarly on capability benchmarks may still produce different mobility curves for the same population.

3. Methods

3.1 Simulation setup

The probe is a simplified extension of the generative agent-based model of Williams et al. williams2023. Two simplifications make each response cleanly attributable to the agent’s persona and the perceived infection level. First, each query is treated as an independent, one-shot decision: where Williams et al. simulated disease spread within an agent population over many days, the present probe samples each agent and infection level directly, with no inter-agent contagion. Second, agent health is held constant — each agent is told “You feel healthy and well.” — rather than evolving with a disease state, so behavioral variation reflects persona and the perceived community-infection rate alone. The town setting (Dewberry Hollow), occupational role, and disease (the fictional Catasat virus) carry over from Williams et al.

3.2 Agent population

The probe uses 100 frozen personas generated by the procedure of Williams et al. williams2023. The five Big Five trait labels john_srivastava1999 are each sampled independently with 50/50 probability between the dimension’s two opposing labels: extraverted or introverted (Extraversion); agreeable or antagonistic (Agreeableness); conscientious or unconscientious (Conscientiousness); emotionally stable or neurotic (Neuroticism); and open to experience or closed to experience (Openness). Ages are drawn between 18 and 65 from a probability distribution that mirrors the 2023 U.S. working-age population popclock. Names and gender are paired by sampling without replacement from the top male and female U.S. names, balanced to produce 50 female and 50 male agents. The full procedure is run with a fixed random seed (42) for reproducibility. Sample demographics are summarized in Figure 1.

3.3 Probe design

Each agent is queried at 40 infection levels with 5 repetitions for each combination of agent and infection level. Infection levels are sampled densely in the low range, with 0.1 percentage-point steps from 0% to 3.5%, then larger steps at 4%, 5%, 6%, and 7%. Each LLM configuration therefore produces 100 × 40 × 5 = 20,000 responses; across 21 configurations the full corpus is 420,000 responses.

3.4 LLM configurations

The 21 configurations were chosen to span six lenses for comparison across the contemporary LLM landscape: provider, reasoning level, model size, model evolution, knowledge cutoff, and release date. Provider is the company that built and offers the LLM; different providers train models on different data corpora, with different alignment techniques, fine-tuning regimes, and architectural choices. Reasoning level is a model-side configuration parameter that controls how much hidden deliberation the model performs before producing its final response; higher levels (off → low → medium → high) typically increase response quality and latency at the cost of more output tokens wei_cot2022. Model size is the relative scale tier of a model within a single provider’s family of releases — for example, Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus in Anthropic’s nomenclature; Lite and Flash in Google’s. Exact parameter counts and training compute are not publicly disclosed for these closed-API models. Model evolution is the sequence of major releases within a single provider’s family over time — for example, GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, and GPT-5.2 from OpenAI; successive releases typically incorporate new training data, refined alignment, and architectural improvements. Knowledge cutoff is the date through which the model’s training data extends; models with later cutoffs have been exposed to more recent text from the public internet and other training corpora. Release date is the date the model became publicly available via API, independent of knowledge cutoff: a model can be released months or years after the cutoff date of its training data.

To support the reasoning-level lens, GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview were each run at four reasoning levels (off, low, medium, high), with each level treated as a separate configuration; all other models were run with reasoning off (or, for OpenAI’s o3, with reasoning required by the API). Data collection across the 21 configurations cost approximately $840 in total API charges (~$464 OpenAI, ~$263 Anthropic, ~$113 Google). Across providers, the configurations span 9 from OpenAI, 5 from Anthropic, and 7 from Google; the full list is given in Table 2.

Sampling temperature is set to 0 for non-reasoning configurations where the API allows it; reasoning-capable models use a temperature of 1 (required by Anthropic; recommended by Google). The OpenAI o-series accepts no temperature parameter.

3.5 Prompt template

Every query uses the same template, identical across the 21 configurations. Each query substitutes four variables into the template: the agent’s first name, the agent’s age in years, the agent’s comma-separated Big Five trait list, and the day’s perceived community-infection rate. The full template is Figure 2.

3.6 Response collection and parsing

Each response is required to be a free-text reasoning passage followed by a single-word yes/no decision, in the format specified at the end of the prompt template (Figure 2). A parser extracts the two components and rejects any response that does not conform; 0 of 420,000 responses required rejection. The reasoning text is preserved for the downstream textual analysis.

3.7 Analytical methods

We perform three analyses on the 420,000 responses, each progressively deeper into the data: (1) Mobility Curve Analysis summarizes each LLM configuration’s aggregate behavior by plotting mobility against infection level and compares curves across configurations with multilinear regression; (2) Persona Analysis examines the agent-level predictors of the stay-home decision (Big Five trait labels, gender, age, and infection level) via two logistic regressions; and (3) Response Analysis investigates the content of the reasoning passage along three dimensions: which concepts the LLM invokes, via mention counts of curated keyword and concept lists; whether mentioning a concept amplifies its behavioral effect, via a third logistic regression that incorporates mention flags as predictors; and how agent-specific the reasoning is, via embedding-based cosine similarity.

3.7.1 Mobility Curve Analysis

For each LLM configuration, the stay-home rate at a given infection level is computed by averaging 500 binary decisions — 5 repetitions for each of the 100 agents. The mobility curve plots mobility (the complement of the stay-home rate, mobility = 1 − stay-home rate) on the y-axis against the 40 sampled infection levels (0 to 3.5% in 0.1 percentage-point steps, plus 4%, 5%, 6%, and 7%) on the x-axis.

To discern differences between mobility curves, we employ multilinear regression in the same style as Williams williams_prompt2023. For each set of configurations being compared (e.g., the four GPT-5.2 reasoning levels), we fit two models. Both take mobility as the dependent variable and use the infection level, the infection level squared, and one dummy variable per non-baseline configuration as predictors; the dummy is set to 1 if the data is from the configuration being tested and 0 if it is from the baseline. The first model, a baseline-shift model, stops there; a statistically significant dummy coefficient indicates that the configuration produces a level shift in the mobility curve relative to the baseline. The second model, a baseline-plus-slope-shift model, additionally includes interactions between each dummy and the infection level; a statistically significant interaction indicates that the configuration’s slope of response to infection level differs from the baseline’s. Per-comparison tables for both models are reported in Appendix A.

3.7.2 Persona Analysis

To examine how agent traits, gender, age, and infection level systematically influence the stay-home decision, two logistic regressions are fit per LLM configuration on the 20,000 individual responses, in the same style as Williams et al. williams2023. The dependent variable is a binary variable of the agent’s decision to stay home (equal to 1 if the agent stays home and 0 otherwise). The fixed-effect logit absorbs each agent’s average tendency to stay home through agent dummies, isolating how the decision responds to changes in infection level. It also provides a pseudo-R² for goodness-of-fit. The random-effect logit replaces the agent dummies with a single random intercept per agent, freeing up the model to estimate trait, gender, age, and infection effects. The reference categories are female, introverted, antagonistic, unconscientious, neurotic, and closed to experience; age is a continuous predictor in years. Per-configuration coefficient tables for both specifications are reported in Appendix B.1; diagnostics — calibration, residual Q-Q, random-intercept normality, BLUPs versus predictors, and residual fit by infection and age — are in Appendix C.

3.7.3 Response Analysis

Each query asks the agent to provide its reasoning before stating its yes/no decision, so the reasoning text likely shapes the decision rather than merely justifying it after the fact. From this text we measure four things in turn: (1) how often LLMs mention specific traits in their reasoning, via keyword counting; (2) whether those mentions amplify the corresponding behavioral effect, via the mention-flag logit; (3) how broader concept categories correlate with the decision direction, via keyword counting; and (4) how agent-specific the reasoning text is, via embedding cosine similarity.

For each Big Five trait label, a keyword set covers the obvious word-form variants of each pole. For example, an extraversion mention is flagged when any of {extroverted, extrovert, extraverted, extravert, extraversion, extroversion, introverted, introvert, introversion} appears in the response. Two additional flags cover infection (keywords like {infection, infected, cases, diagnosed} or any number followed by “%” or “percent”) and age (keywords like {years old, young, age, old}, or the agent’s own age number). All flags are binary per response and case-insensitive.

These mention flags then enter a third logistic regression — the mention-flag logit — which extends the random-effects logit from §3.7.2 with one binary indicator per Big Five trait label, plus one for infection and one for age. A flag enters the model only when its overall mention rate falls within [15%, 85%]; outside this window the flag carries no usable variation. The mention-flag coefficients answer whether mentioning a concept amplifies or dampens its behavioral effect. Per-configuration coefficient tables are in Appendix B.2; diagnostics are in Appendix C.

A separate, broader concept dictionary covers five themes: Work, Community, Self Health, Virus Properties, and Virus Prevalence. These categories were drawn from the prompt template (Figure 2), which mentions going to the office for work, feeling healthy and well, an airborne virus that spreads from human to human, a daily new-infection rate, and the town of Dewberry Hollow. Keywords for each concept were then selected via manual inspection of random response samples across all 21 configurations. Like the trait flags, concept-category flags are binary per response and case-insensitive.

Lastly, for agent individuation, every reasoning passage is embedded with OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model oai_embeddings (3,072 dimensions). Two questions follow, both restricted to agents whose 5 repetitions all returned the same decision (the “5/5 agreement” subset). The first: how similar are an agent’s own 5 responses? Within-agent similarity is computed as the mean of the 10 pairwise cosines among an agent’s 5 reps, then averaged across all qualifying 5/5 agreements for each LLM. The second: how similar are two different agents who reach the same decision at the same infection level? Across-agent similarity is computed bucket by bucket (one bucket per infection level and decision direction): every pair of qualifying agents in the bucket contributes the mean of the 25 rep-pair cosines between them, and the result is averaged across all such pairs.

4. Results

This section presents the results of the three analyses introduced in §3.7. Within each subsection, results are presented in the paper order described in Table 1.

4.1 Mobility Curve Results

The 21 mobility curves vary dramatically: average stay-home rates span nearly the full 0–100% range across the 21 configurations. The six subsections below examine variation across LLMs from six different angles. §4.1.1 examines the role of the LLM provider; §4.1.2 the role of reasoning level; §4.1.3 the role of model size within a provider family; §4.1.4 the role of model evolution within a family across time; §4.1.5 the role of knowledge cutoff; and §4.1.6 the role of release date.

4.1.1 Cross-provider differences (RQ1) — Does LLM provider affect mobility curves?

The mobility-curve landscape within each provider is itself wide. Figure 3 overlays all configurations from each of the three providers, showing the spread of mobility responses across the configurations included in this study. Within OpenAI alone, mobility ranges from near-100% stay-home (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-5.2 off) to near-0% stay-home (GPT-4o); within Anthropic, from Claude 3 Haiku at the cautious end to Claude Haiku 4.5 at the mobile end; within Google, the spread is narrower but still spans the lower half of the mobility range. There is no single “Anthropic look” or “OpenAI look” to a mobility curve once different models within each provider are considered.

To make a controlled cross-provider comparison, we hold model size (flagship within each provider, as of the December 2025 data-collection window) and reasoning level (off) constant. Figure 4 shows the resulting flagship mobility curves: GPT-5.2 (OpenAI), Claude Opus 4.5 (Anthropic), and Gemini 3 Flash Preview (Google). All three start near 100% mobility at 0% infection, but the curves diverge sharply as soon as any community infection is reported. GPT-5.2 becomes cautious almost immediately — its mobility drops to near zero by 0.3% infection and stays there across the rest of the range. Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview, in contrast, behave as relatively mobile agents across the full range; their mobility hovers in the 75–90% band and declines only gradually as infection rises. The multilinear regression confirms this pattern: GPT-5.2 differs significantly from the other two flagships, while Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview are not statistically distinguishable from each other (Appendix A).

The two figures together suggest that the choice of LLM matters along multiple dimensions. Within each provider, model variants span a wide mobility range; across providers, even matched flagships occupy distinct regions of the mobility space. For a researcher building a generative-agent simulation, neither “pick any provider” nor “pick the flagship” produces interchangeable behavior.

4.1.2 Reasoning level (RQ2) — Does LLM reasoning level affect mobility curves?

Reasoning level is a configurable LLM parameter controlling how much hidden deliberation the model performs before producing its final response. Two configurations span the full reasoning-level ladder (off, low, medium, high): GPT-5.2 (OpenAI) and Gemini 3 Flash Preview (Google). Figure 5 places these two reasoning ladders side by side, with each panel showing how the same model’s mobility curve responds to changes in reasoning level.

For GPT-5.2, increasing reasoning shifts the mobility curve modestly upward — mobility rises by roughly 9 percentage points from reasoning off to reasoning high. For Gemini 3 Flash Preview, the direction is opposite: enabling any non-zero reasoning level shifts the curve downward by roughly 9 percentage points relative to reasoning off, with little additional change between low, medium, and high. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

Reasoning is a real but minor lever, and its sign differs between the two LLMs tested. Both shifts are roughly 9 percentage points and small compared to the cross-provider gap reported in §4.1.1. The result complicates any general claim that more reasoning makes a generative agent uniformly more or less cautious.

4.1.3 Model size (RQ3) — Does LLM size affect mobility curves?

Model size refers to a provider’s relative scale tier within a single family of releases. Two providers in the data have multiple model sizes within a single generation: Anthropic’s Claude 4.5 family (Haiku [small], Sonnet [middle], Opus [large]) and Google’s Gemini 2.5 family (Flash Lite [small], Flash [middle]). All five configurations are evaluated with reasoning off. Figure 6 places the two size ladders side by side.

For the Anthropic Claude 4.5 family, mobility does not change monotonically with size. Going from Haiku (small) to Sonnet (middle) shifts the curve substantially downward — Sonnet sits well below Haiku across the full infection range. But going from Sonnet to Opus (large) reverses direction: Opus tracks close to Haiku rather than continuing further down. For the Google Gemini 2.5 family, Gemini 2.5 Flash sits well above Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite across most of the infection range. Flash Lite itself shows an unusual within-curve pattern — its mobility starts moderate at 0% infection, dips sharply between 0.1% and 0.9%, jumps back at 1.0%, and stabilizes above 50% from 1.2% onwards. One possible explanation is that Flash Lite, a smaller and lower-capacity variant of Gemini 2.5 Flash, is oversensitive to sub-1% infection levels; it reacts more strongly to fractional values in the 0.1–0.9% range than to values at or above 1%. Other small models in this study (Claude 3 Haiku, Claude Haiku 4.5) do not show this dip-and-recover pattern, so size alone does not fully account for the behavior. Across both providers’ size ladders, the data shows substantial variation but no consistent direction of effect attributable to size. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

4.1.4 Model evolution (RQ4) — Does LLM evolution affect mobility curves?

Model evolution refers to the sequence of major releases within a single provider’s family over time, where each successive release typically incorporates new training data, refined alignment, and architectural improvements. All three providers contribute model evolution data: Anthropic from Claude Sonnet 4.0 to Claude Sonnet 4.5; OpenAI from GPT-3.5 Turbo through GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, and GPT-5.2; and Google from Gemini 2.0 Flash through Gemini 2.5 Flash to Gemini 3 Flash Preview. All configurations are evaluated with reasoning off. Figure 7 places the three evolution sequences side by side.

The Anthropic sequence has only two points: Claude Sonnet 4.5 sits modestly above Claude Sonnet 4.0 across the infection range. The OpenAI sequence shows wide swings between successive releases — GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-5.2 sit near the cautious end of the mobility range; GPT-4o sits at the mobile end; GPT-4.1 and GPT-5.1 sit at intermediate positions. Going from one release to the next does not move the OpenAI curve in a consistent direction. The Google sequence shows a large upward shift from Gemini 2.0 Flash to Gemini 2.5 Flash, then almost no further change between Gemini 2.5 Flash and Gemini 3 Flash Preview. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

Across the three providers, model evolution does not produce a consistent direction of effect on mobility, and the within-provider patterns differ from each other.

4.1.5 Knowledge cutoff (RQ5) — Does LLM knowledge cutoff affect mobility curves?

Knowledge cutoff is the date through which the model’s training data extends; models with later cutoffs have been exposed to more recent text from the public internet. The 21 configurations span knowledge cutoffs from September 2021 (GPT-3.5 Turbo) to August 2025 (Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.2). Figure 8 plots each configuration on a knowledge-cutoff timeline, showing the four era groupings used in the analysis below: pre-2024, mid-2024 (Jun–Sep), early 2025 (Jan–Mar), and late 2025 (Aug). Figure 9 then shows the mobility curves within each era.

Within each era panel, the curves do not cluster by knowledge cutoff. Models with the same or near-identical training-data cutoff date land at very different positions on the mobility axis: in the pre-2024 panel, GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4o sit at opposite ends of the mobility range despite cutoffs only two years apart; in the mid-2024 panel, four configurations with cutoffs in a 3-month window (Jun–Sep 2024) span nearly the full mobility range; in the early-2025 panel, six configurations with cutoffs within a 3-month window again span the full range; in the late-2025 panel, Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.2 share an August 2025 cutoff but produce distinctly different curves. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

Knowledge cutoff does not predict mobility curve placement in this data. Whatever drives the variation across LLMs reported in §4.1.1, it is not the recency of training data alone.

4.1.6 Release date (RQ6) — Does LLM release date affect mobility curves?

Release date is the date the model was made publicly available via API; it is generally independent of the model’s knowledge cutoff. The 21 configurations span release dates from January 2024 (GPT-3.5 Turbo) to December 2025 (GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview). Figure 10 plots each configuration on a release-date timeline, showing the three era groupings used in the analysis below: legacy (pre-2025), early 2025 (Feb–Jun), and late 2025 (Sep–Dec). Figure 11 then shows the mobility curves within each era.

As with knowledge cutoff, configurations sharing a release-date era do not cluster on the mobility axis. The legacy panel shows GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4o at opposite ends of the range despite release dates within a year of each other. The early-2025 panel shows six configurations released within a five-month window spanning the full mobility range. The late-2025 panel shows six configurations released within a four-month window also spanning a wide range. The multilinear regression in Appendix A reports which configurations differ significantly.

Release date does not predict mobility curve placement in this data, and does not explain the variation across LLMs observed in §4.1.1.

4.2 Persona Results

The four subsections below examine the agent-level predictors of the stay-home decision and how the 21 LLMs vary in their treatment of those predictors. §4.2.1 examines the direction of each persona predictor’s effect (RQ7); §4.2.2 ranks persona predictors by their relative importance within and across LLMs (RQ8); §4.2.3 the magnitude of all persona effects combined relative to the infection-level effect (RQ9); and §4.2.4 agreement across LLMs on which agent personas are most cautious (RQ10).

4.2.1 Trait effect directions (RQ7) — What is the direction of each persona predictor’s effect on the stay-home decision?

Agent traits enter the random-effects logit (§3.7.2) as predictors: five Big Five trait dummies, a male dummy, and age in years. Each Big Five dummy equals 1 for the named pole (extraverted, agreeable, conscientious, emotionally stable, open to experience) and 0 for its opposite (introverted, antagonistic, unconscientious, neurotic, closed to experience). The male dummy is 1 for male and 0 for female. Age is a continuous predictor; its coefficient is per year, and is scaled by 47 (the 18–65 span) for magnitude comparisons against the binary traits. Each predictor’s β gives the log-odds shift in stay-home probability associated with the dummy = 1 value, holding the other predictors fixed. A negative β on a trait dummy means agents at that pole are more mobile, and a positive β means they stay home more.

Figure 12 shows the per-predictor β coefficients across all 21 LLM configurations, one panel per predictor, with significance markers and 95% confidence intervals. The right column of each panel reports a normalized |β| / infection-range ratio, where infection range is the maximum log-odds shift the infection signal produces over the 0–7% range.

For four of the five Big Five dimensions, every LLM agrees on the sign of the coefficient. Extraversion is negative in all 21 configurations (significant in 20). Agreeableness is positive in all 21 (significant in 18). Conscientiousness is positive in all 21 (significant in 19). Emotional stability is negative in 20 of 21 (significant in 19). The unanimity holds within each provider: all 5 Anthropic, all 9 OpenAI, and all 7 Gemini configurations sign these four traits in the same direction.

Openness to experience is the least consistent Big Five dimension: 14 of 21 configurations show a significant positive effect, and only one (Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite) shows a significant negative effect. Gender (male) is significant in only 3 of 21 configurations and varies in direction across configurations. Age (scaled across the 18–65 range) is significant in 6 of 21 configurations, mostly within OpenAI.

4.2.2 Predictor importance (RQ8) — Which persona predictors most strongly drive the stay-home decision?

To rank persona predictors by influence within and across LLMs, each predictor’s |β| is divided by that LLM’s infection range (§4.2.1). A ratio above 1× means the predictor alone shifts the decision more than infection’s full effect. Figure 13 plots each configuration’s predictor ratios on one row, with a vertical 1× line marking the threshold.

Gemini configurations sit furthest right. Four of the five Big Five traits have median ratios above 1× (extraversion 1.83, conscientiousness 1.40, emotional stability 1.35, openness 1.09; only agreeableness sits just below at 0.93), meaning each of those four traits typically exceeds infection’s full effect on its own. Anthropic configurations cluster lower, with median ratios spanning a wide range (conscientiousness 0.75, extraversion 0.64, emotional stability 0.61, agreeableness 0.27, openness 0.04); across all five configurations and five Big Five traits, only one ratio crosses 1× (Claude Opus 4.5’s conscientiousness, at 1.09×). OpenAI configurations cluster lowest, with all five Big Five median ratios below 0.4× (extraversion 0.35, emotional stability 0.24, conscientiousness 0.19, agreeableness 0.17, openness 0.15); no OpenAI trait coefficient anywhere in the figure exceeds 1×, indicating that for OpenAI configurations trait identity is a smaller share of the decision than that of infection level. Across all three providers, gender and age (diamonds) sit near zero on the ratio axis, consistent with their low significance counts in §4.2.1.

The rank-1 predictor (the predictor with the largest |β| / infection ratio) within each configuration also splits along provider lines. In all 9 OpenAI configurations, infection level is the rank-1 predictor. In 4 of 5 Anthropic configurations, infection is rank-1; only Claude Opus 4.5 promotes a Big Five trait (conscientiousness) to rank-1. In 6 of 7 Gemini configurations a Big Five trait is rank-1 (extraversion in 4 configurations, emotional stability in 2); only Gemini 2.0 Flash keeps infection at rank-1. The disagreement on which predictor matters most is itself a provider-level pattern: OpenAI is uniformly infection-dominated, Gemini is uniformly trait-dominated, and Anthropic is mostly infection-dominated with a single Opus exception.

4.2.3 Persona vs. infection magnitude (RQ9) — Are personality effects larger than infection effects?

The persona-to-infection ratio compares total persona influence against the infection effect. The denominator is the same infection range used in §4.2.1 and §4.2.2; the numerator, the personality range, is the maximum gap in stay-home log-odds achievable by varying an agent’s persona (Big Five traits, gender, age) at zero infection — a sum of |β| across all persona predictors from the random-effects logit. A ratio above 1× means persona variation alone shifts the decision more than infection’s full effect. Figure 14 plots both ranges on a common log-odds scale: the solid line spans the personality range at zero infection, and the dashed line shifts the same range by the maximum infection contribution.

Across the 21 configurations, 19 have a persona-to-infection ratio above 1× and the median ratio is 2.75×. The provider-level pattern parallels the per-trait pattern in §4.2.1. Gemini configurations are the most persona-dominated, with a median ratio of 7.96×: five of the seven Gemini configurations (Gemini 2.5 Flash plus all four Gemini 3 Flash Preview reasoning levels) reach ratios between 7.07× and 8.85×, while the older Gemini 2.0 Flash and Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite sit lower at 2.93× and 2.75×. Anthropic configurations are intermediate, with a median of 2.84× and a spread from 1.17× (Claude 3 Haiku) to 3.67× (Claude Opus 4.5). OpenAI configurations are the most infection-dominated, with a median of 1.37× and most configurations between 1× and 2×; GPT-4.1 (3.09×) sits noticeably above the OpenAI median, and two configurations sit below 1× (GPT-5.2 off at 0.08×, GPT-5.2 low at 0.81×).

Beyond the persona-to-infection ratio, Figure 14 also shows where each LLM’s personality range sits on the absolute log-odds scale at 0% infection. For 19 of the 21 configurations, the personality range crosses the 50% P(stay-home) threshold: the most-cautious persona sits above 50% and the most-mobile persona sits below, with infection then shifting the range further toward stay-home. Two configurations are exceptions, both with personality ranges entirely below 50%: Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-4o. For these two LLMs, every persona is inclined to go out at 0% infection regardless of trait combination. No configuration has a personality range sitting entirely above 50% — at zero infection, every LLM produces at least one persona that would go out.

4.2.4 Agent identity agreement (RQ10) — Do LLMs agree on which agent personas are most cautious?

For each LLM configuration, the 100 agents are independently ranked by their stay-home rate within that configuration — the fraction of the agent’s 200 decisions (40 infection levels × 5 reps) on which the agent chose to stay home. Rank 1 is the most cautious persona under that LLM. Ties are resolved by average-rank tie handling. Each agent then has 21 ranks, one for each LLM configuration. Figure 15 summarizes each agent’s 21 ranks: the dot is the median rank the agent received, the dark bar spans the IQR (25th–75th percentile), and the pale whisker spans the full range. Agents are sorted top-to-bottom by median rank.

Agreement among LLMs is uneven across rank positions. The median IQR among the top-25 most-cautious agents is 16 ranks; among the bottom-25 most-mobile agents, 14 ranks; but among the middle 50 agents it widens to 26 ranks. LLMs converge more readily on agents at the extremes than on agents in the middle of the cautious-to-mobile distribution. The median full range echoes this pattern — 48 ranks among the top 25, 62 among the bottom 25, and 68 among the middle 50. No agent escapes substantial outlier disagreement: the smallest range across the 21 LLMs is 27 ranks, and the median agent has a 66-rank range.

4.3 Response Results

The four subsections below examine the content of the reasoning passage. §4.3.1 (RQ11) reports how often each LLM mentions Big Five trait, infection, and age concepts; §4.3.2 (RQ12) tests whether, conditional on a mention, the mention amplifies or contradicts the trait’s behavioral effect; §4.3.3 (RQ13) examines which concept categories are cited in stay-home versus go-out reasoning; and §4.3.4 (RQ14) measures how agent-specific each LLM’s reasoning text is.

4.3.1 Mention rates (RQ11) — How often do LLMs mention trait, infection, and age concepts in their reasoning?

A keyword dictionary (§3.7.3) tags each response for mentions of the five Big Five trait labels, the agent’s infection level, and the agent’s age. A trait counts as mentioned whenever a keyword from either pole appears (e.g., either “introverted” or “extraverted” registers as an extraversion mention). Figure 16 reports the resulting per-LLM mention rates, broken out by the agent’s assigned Big Five pole. Cells outside the [15%, 85%] gate are dimmed: at those rates the corresponding mention flag carries no usable variation and is excluded from the mention-flag logit used in §4.3.2.

Mention rates vary widely both within and across providers (Figure 16). Among the Big Five labels, conscientiousness is mentioned most often (mean 55.8% across the 21 configurations) and openness least (mean 16.6%). Provider-level differences are substantial: average Big Five mention rate is 57.7% across the five Anthropic configurations, 17.1% across the nine OpenAI configurations, and 48.7% across the seven Gemini configurations. Infection level is mentioned in nearly every response on most LLMs (mean rate 87.8%) and falls outside the 15–85% gate for most of them, so the infection mention flag enters the §4.3.2 regression for only 4 of 21 configurations. Age is rarely cited anywhere (mean 4.0% across configurations). GPT-3.5 Turbo is an outlier on every dimension, mentioning infection in only 0.2% of responses and Big Five labels in under 2%.

4.3.2 Mention amplification (RQ12) — When a trait is mentioned, does the mention amplify or contradict the trait’s behavioral effect?

The mention-flag logit (§3.7.3) extends the random-effects spec from §4.2 with one binary flag per Big Five trait label, plus one for infection and one for age. A flag enters the model only when its overall mention rate from Figure 16 falls in [15%, 85%], for a total of twelve mention coefficients reported below: ten Big Five pole flags plus the infection and age flags. Each mention coefficient is the log-odds shift in stay-home probability when its concept is mentioned. A positive coefficient indicates the mention pushes the decision toward staying home and a negative coefficient toward going out. Figure 17 plots those coefficients in absolute log-odds.

For Extraversion, mentioning “introverted” shifts behavior toward stay-home in 9 of 12 configurations (1 go-out, 2 not significant); mentioning “extraverted” shifts toward go-out in 9 of 11 (2 not significant). Introverted agents who mention their trait stay home even more; extraverted agents who mention theirs go out even more.

For Agreeableness, mentioning “antagonistic” shifts toward go-out in 10 of 11 configurations (1 stay-home); mentioning “agreeable” is mixed — 5 stay-home, 4 go-out, 2 not significant out of 11. Antagonistic agents who mention their trait go out even more; agreeable agents who mention theirs split roughly evenly on direction.

For Conscientiousness, mentioning “unconscientious” shifts toward go-out in 8 of 13 configurations (2 stay-home, 3 not significant); mentioning “conscientious” shifts toward go-out in 9 of 11 (2 not significant). Unconscientious agents who mention their trait go out even more, following the trait coefficient. Conscientious agents who mention theirs also shift toward going out — the lone paradox among the Big Five, opposite to the trait coefficient.

For Neuroticism, mentioning “neurotic” shifts toward stay-home in all 12 configurations; mentioning “emotionally stable” shifts toward go-out in all 12. Neurotic agents who mention their trait stay home even more; emotionally stable agents who mention theirs go out even more.

For Openness, mentioning “closed” shifts toward go-out in all 7 estimable configurations; mentioning “open” is split 3 stay-home, 3 go-out, 4 not significant out of 10. Closed agents who mention their trait go out even more; open agents who mention theirs are mixed across LLMs.

The infection flag is estimable in only 4 of 21 configurations; the rest fall outside the 15–85% gate because nearly every response mentions infection. In all 4 it is significantly stay-home: explicit mention of infection makes the stay-home decision more likely beyond what the infection level alone predicts. The age flag is estimable in only one configuration (Gemini 2.5 Flash), where it points toward going out; with a single estimable LLM the pattern is too thin to generalize.

4.3.3 Decision themes (RQ13) — Which concept categories are cited in stay-home vs. go-out reasoning?

Sections 4.3.1 and 4.3.2 describe how often each concept is mentioned and whether mentioning a trait amplifies its behavioral effect. This subsection asks a complementary question: are concepts cited more often in stay-home or in go-out reasoning? Figure 18 reports this for the ten Big Five trait labels plus infection and age. Each cell is the mention rate among that subgroup’s stay-home responses minus the mention rate among its go-out responses, in percentage points (pp). For example, Claude Opus 4.5’s Introverted column reads +16 pp: 78% of Opus 4.5’s introverted agents mention an extraversion keyword when staying home, versus 62% when going out.

Per §4.2.1, agents at the cautious-coded poles (introverted, agreeable, conscientious, neurotic, and open to experience) tend to stay home more, while their mobile counterparts (extraverted, antagonistic, unconscientious, emotionally stable, and closed to experience) go out more. Figure 18’s median row mostly aligns with these trait directions. Among the cautious-coded poles: introverted is cited a median 16 pp more often in stay-home responses, agreeable and conscientious show no skew (both 0 pp), and neurotic is cited 20 pp more in stay-home; open to experience is the surprising exception at −2 pp, slightly leaning go-out, matching the mixed pattern §4.3.2 flagged for the open pole. Among the mobile-coded poles: extraverted is cited 10 pp more in go-out responses, antagonistic 9 pp more, unconscientious 10 pp more, emotionally stable 13 pp more, and closed to experience 8 pp more. Infection and age each lean slightly toward stay-home (+2 pp and +1 pp).

Figure 19 extends Figure 18’s Δ comparison to a broader keyword set (§3.7.3) covering Work, Community, Self Health, Virus Properties, and Virus Prevalence — categories chosen because each one is directly cued by the prompt template (Figure 2).

Concept categories show much sharper stay-vs-go asymmetries than trait labels. For stay-home reasoning, Community is cited a median 44 pp more often than in go-out, followed by Virus Properties at +20 pp and Virus Prevalence at +9 pp. On the go-out side, Self Health is cited a median 37 pp more often in go-out reasoning, with Work obligations 7 pp behind. Stay-home reasoning skews toward other-focused concepts (Community, Virus Properties); go-out reasoning skews toward self-focused concepts (Self Health, Work).

4.3.4 Agent individuation (RQ14) — How agent-specific is each LLM’s reasoning text?

Sections 4.3.1–4.3.3 measure which specific concepts agents cite and how those mentions correlate with each decision. This subsection asks a higher-level question: how much does the reasoning text itself vary across agents? The agent-individuation analysis (§3.7.3) embeds every reasoning passage and, restricted to the “5/5 agreement” subset, computes two cosine summaries per LLM: within-agent similarity (the mean cosine across an agent’s 5 reps) and across-agent similarity (the mean cosine between agents who reached the same decision at the same infection level). The gap Δ = (within − across) measures agent individuation: a large positive Δ means the LLM produces text more similar within an agent than across agents (agent-specific reasoning); a near-zero Δ means similar text across different agents (templated text).

Cosine similarity values can be hard to interpret in isolation. Figure 20 anchors the scale with three example response pairs from Claude Opus 4.5 at infection 3.5%, all at the same go-out decision: a near-paraphrase pair from one agent’s two reps (≈ 0.99), a mid-similarity pair from two different agents (≈ 0.85), and a low-similarity pair from two different agents whose go-out reasoning diverges (≈ 0.65).

Figure 21 reports the within−across gap (Δ) per LLM. Each row shows the within-agent (filled) and across-agent (hollow) cosine values connected by a line; rows are grouped by provider, and marker color encodes the LLM’s sampling temperature (blue = 0, orange = 1).

All 21 LLMs have a positive Δ, meaning every LLM produces at least somewhat agent-specific reasoning, though magnitudes vary considerably. Gemini configurations show the largest gap (median Δ = 0.13; range 0.11 to 0.24), Anthropic is intermediate (median 0.12; range 0.08 to 0.16), and OpenAI sits lowest (median 0.04; range 0.01 to 0.16). Within OpenAI, reasoning-enabled configurations produce notably more templated text than reasoning-off ones: the GPT-5.2 ladder yields Δ between 0.01 and 0.03, o3 sits at 0.04, while GPT-4o (Δ = 0.16) and GPT-4.1 (Δ = 0.15) reach the upper end of the OpenAI range. The provider ordering (Gemini » Anthropic » OpenAI) parallels the persona-effect-magnitude ordering from §4.2.2 and §4.2.3: LLMs that weight persona inputs more heavily in their decisions also produce more agent-specific reasoning text.

5. Discussion

The Discussion takes up the three results subsections in turn: §5.1 the mobility-curve findings (RQ1–RQ6), §5.2 the persona-effect findings (RQ7–RQ10), and §5.3 the reasoning-text findings (RQ11–RQ14). §5.4 distills the cross-cutting implications for GABM practitioners, and §5.5 records the study’s limitations.

5.1 Mobility Curve Discussion (RQ1–RQ6)

The 21 mobility curves span nearly the full 0–100% stay-home range, but the practical reading for a researcher building a generative-agent simulation is narrow: neither “pick any provider” nor “pick the flagship” produces interchangeable behavior (RQ1). At the matched flagships-at-reasoning-off comparison (Figure 4), GPT-5.2 collapses to near-zero mobility almost immediately, while Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Flash Preview remain in the 75–90% mobility band across the infection range and are statistically indistinguishable from each other (Appendix A). Within each provider, model variants themselves span a wide mobility range comparable to the across-provider gap (Figure 3).

Of the five within-family lenses, only reasoning level (RQ2) produces a measurable shift, and even that effect is small and direction-ambiguous. Reasoning level moves mobility by roughly 9 percentage points (Figure 5), with opposite signs in the two configurations sampled across the full ladder: increasing reasoning makes GPT-5.2 more mobile and Gemini 3 Flash Preview less mobile. A practitioner can expect reasoning level to nudge generative-agent behavior, but cannot predict the direction of that nudge without testing the specific model.

The remaining four lenses do not generate actionable insight. Model size within a family (RQ3), model evolution within a provider (RQ4), knowledge cutoff (RQ5), and release date (RQ6) all fail to predict mobility-curve placement (Figures 6–11). Configurations that share any of these properties still land at very different positions on the mobility axis. Same-family models at different sizes, successive releases within a family, models with knowledge cutoffs in the same three-month window, and models released within a four-month window each span much of the 0–100% range. A researcher cannot predict an LLM’s mobility curve from its size, generation, knowledge cutoff, or release date. Identifying what within an LLM’s training pipeline produces a particular mobility tendency — alignment objectives, the RLHF system, system-prompt conventions, etc. — is left as future work.

5.2 Persona Discussion (RQ7–RQ10)

The persona analysis shows two complementary patterns: directions agree across LLMs, magnitudes do not. For four of the five Big Five traits, every LLM agrees on the direction of the effect (RQ7, Figure 12). 21 of 21 agree that extraverted agents are more mobile and that agreeable and conscientious agents stay home more, and 20 of 21 agree that emotionally stable agents are more mobile. Openness is the lone Big Five inconsistency, mostly positive (14 of 21 significantly positive, 1 significantly negative). Gender and age rarely reach significance. The unanimity holds within each provider as well as across providers. A plausible reading is that LLMs have inherited a shared mapping from Big Five labels to risk-related behavior, learned from broadly similar training corpora. The direction-agreement parallels Mooney et al.’s finding mooney2025 that agents’ surface-level responses align with stated profiles across nine models from three model families. This is reassuring for GABM epidemic model realism: swapping the LLM does not typically change which traits make a generative agent more cautious.

The importance of each predictor (RQ8, Figure 13), in contrast, splits sharply along provider lines. In OpenAI, infection level is the top predictor in all 9 configurations, and no Big Five trait coefficient anywhere exceeds the infection coefficient. In Anthropic, infection is the top predictor in 4 of 5; Claude Opus 4.5 is the exception (conscientiousness). In Gemini, a Big Five trait is the top predictor in 6 of 7 configurations (extraversion in four, emotional stability in two). In fact, four of the five Gemini Big Five median trait coefficients exceed infection (extraversion, conscientiousness, emotional stability, and openness).

For most LLMs, the combined persona effect, summed across all persona predictors at once (Big Five, gender, age), outweighs the community-infection signal in driving the stay-home decision (RQ9, Figure 14). 19 of 21 configurations show a personality range (the total log-odds gap from varying all persona predictors) exceeding the infection range, with a median ratio of 2.75×. The pattern is not universal: the two GPT-5.2 settings at off and low reasoning are infection-dominated. These are also the configurations where mobility collapses to near zero across the full infection range (Figure 5), suggesting that when an LLM is maximally cautious to the infection signal it crowds the persona signal out. The provider-level spread (Gemini median 7.96×, Anthropic 2.84×, OpenAI 1.37×) means the relative weight of persona versus environment in driving agent decisions differs roughly six-fold across providers. A simulation built on a Gemini backbone will exhibit more persona-driven heterogeneity in agent behavior than the same simulation built on an OpenAI backbone, even with identical agent population, prompt, and infection signal.

Agreement among LLMs on agent identity (RQ10, Figure 15) is uneven across the cautious-to-mobile distribution. At the extremes, LLMs agree more closely: the median IQR among the 25 most-cautious agents is 16 ranks, and among the 25 most-mobile is 14 ranks; in the middle the IQR widens to 26 ranks. But the agreement is only relative. No agent escapes substantial outlier disagreement: the most-agreed-upon agent has a 27-rank gap between its highest and lowest LLM ranks, and the median agent has a 66-rank range. This pattern is consistent with the trait-direction finding above. Agents whose profiles stack many cautious-coded traits (or many mobile-coded traits) get ranked similarly on every LLM because the direction of each trait’s effect is universal. Agents with mixed trait profiles are sensitive to which traits a given LLM weighs more heavily, and that weighting is itself a provider-level pattern.

5.3 Response Discussion (RQ11–RQ14)

LLMs differ sharply in how often they cite trait, infection, and age concepts in their reasoning (RQ11, Figure 16). Average Big Five mention rate is 57.7% across the five Anthropic configurations, 48.7% across the seven Gemini configurations, and 17.1% across the nine OpenAI configurations. Most LLMs cite infection in nearly every response (mean 87.8%), age rarely (mean 4.0%), and GPT-3.5 Turbo is an outlier on every dimension (under 2% on Big Five labels, 0.2% on infection). The citation ordering Anthropic » Gemini » OpenAI does not match the persona-effect-magnitude ordering Gemini » Anthropic » OpenAI from §5.2. Gemini weighs trait identity most heavily in its decisions but surfaces trait language less often in its text than Anthropic does. With that being said, mentioning a trait and weighting a trait are not the same lever: a model can write less about persona while still letting persona drive the decision more.

When a trait is mentioned, the mention usually amplifies the trait’s behavioral direction (RQ12, Figure 17). For 8 of the 10 Big Five trait labels, the significant mention shifts align with the trait’s direction in the persona regression: introverted agents who name their trait stay home even more, extraverted agents who name theirs go out even more, etc. Openness and conscientiousness are the two exceptions. For openness, the “open” mention is genuinely split: 3 configurations shift toward staying home, 3 toward going out, and 4 produce no significant shift out of 10 estimable. “Open to experience” can support either decision in pandemic reasoning: an agent might write “I’m open to learning more about this, so let me stay home and follow guidance” or “I’m open to new experiences, so let me go out.” Conscientiousness produces the lone systematic paradox: mentioning “conscientious” shifts the log-odds toward going out in 9 of 11 estimable configurations, opposite the direction of the conscientious-stay-home coefficient. A plausible reading is that conscientiousness gets invoked instrumentally rather than as a stay-home marker. For example, Claude Opus 4.5’s agent Jaime, while going out, writes “I’m conscientious about my work responsibilities and need to go to the office” (Figure 20).

Concepts cited in stay-home reasoning differ systematically from those cited in go-out reasoning (RQ13). For trait labels (Figure 18), the median pattern roughly tracks each trait’s direction in the persona regression: cautious-coded labels (introverted, neurotic) are cited more often in stay-home responses, and mobile-coded labels (extraverted, antagonistic, unconscientious, emotionally stable, closed) more often in go-out responses. Open to experience is the exception, leaning slightly go-out (−2 pp) despite being cautious-coded. This matches the mixed open-mention pattern from RQ12 and openness’s role as the most inconsistent Big Five direction in §5.2 (RQ7).

Beyond trait labels, broader concept categories show much sharper asymmetries (Figure 19). Community is cited a median 44 pp more often in stay-home reasoning than in go-out, and Virus Properties (+20 pp) and Virus Prevalence (+9 pp) reinforce the stay-home skew. Self Health is cited 37 pp more often in go-out reasoning, with Work obligations 7 pp behind. Stay-home reasoning skews toward other-focused concepts (Community, Virus Properties); go-out reasoning skews toward self-focused concepts (Self Health, Work).

All 21 LLMs produce at least somewhat agent-specific reasoning text (RQ14, Figure 21): every configuration shows a positive within−across cosine gap (Δ). Magnitudes vary in a familiar provider-level pattern. Gemini configurations show the largest gap (median Δ = 0.13; range 0.11 to 0.24), Anthropic is intermediate (median 0.12; range 0.08 to 0.16), and OpenAI sits lowest (median 0.04; range 0.01 to 0.16). The provider ordering Gemini » Anthropic » OpenAI parallels the persona-effect-magnitude ordering from §5.2: LLMs that weight persona inputs more heavily in their decisions also produce more agent-specific reasoning text.

Within OpenAI, reasoning-enabled configurations produce notably more templated text than reasoning-off ones: the GPT-5.2 ladder yields Δ between 0.01 and 0.03, o3 sits at 0.04, while reasoning-off GPT-4o (0.16) and GPT-4.1 (0.15) reach the upper end of the OpenAI range. This tracks the persona-vs-infection finding from §5.2 (RQ9): the reasoning-on GPT-5.2 configurations are also the most infection-dominated in OpenAI.

5.4 Implications for GABM design

GABM was introduced to replace explicit behavioral parameterization ghaffarzadegan2024. Agents derive their decisions from a well-trained LLM instead of from modeler-coded decision rules. The variation across LLMs documented here gives that move a sharper edge: the LLM choice itself becomes the parameterization. This empirically reinforces recent concerns about generative-ABM validation larooij_tornberg2026, liwu2025 and aligns with a growing body of shallow behavioral probes showing that LLM choice measurably moves agent output dominguez_olmedo2024, einwiller2025, mooney2025, parikh2026. Four practical implications follow for researchers building generative-agent simulations.

The choice of LLM is not interchangeable. Aggregate mobility, the magnitude of persona effects, the relative weight of persona versus infection signal, and the agent-specificity of reasoning text all depend on the LLM.

No pattern within a provider’s lineup predicts agent behavior. The lenses practitioners typically use to choose between models — bigger model, newer release, more recent training data, more reasoning — do not, in this domain, predict generative-agent behavior. A researcher who upgrades from one model to its successor, scales to a larger model in the same family, or enables reasoning should not assume agent behavior will change in a known direction.

Persona inputs generalize directionally but not in magnitude. Assigning Big Five trait labels to agents will reliably push behavior in expected directions on every LLM tested. But the magnitude of those effects varies several-fold across providers and is not predictable in advance for any specific LLM.

The reasoning text is real signal, not post-hoc justification. When an agent mentions a trait or invokes a particular decision theme (§5.3), those textual choices correlate with the decision the agent makes. For GABM researchers, this argues for retaining and analyzing the reasoning text rather than discarding it.

5.5 Limitations

Several limitations of this study point to natural extensions. The LLM sample, while spanning three major providers and 21 distinct configurations, is small relative to the broader population of available models. Additionally, the rapidly-evolving LLM landscape means today’s findings are tied to a particular snapshot of each provider’s training pipeline.

The probe is also monetarily intensive. The 420,000 decision queries plus the embedding step for §4.3.4 generated tens of thousands of API calls per LLM and amounted to roughly $840 in API spend. As LLM costs continue to fall and local-running models become more capable, the cost barrier to probe studies of this scale should ease, but for now it remains a meaningful constraint.

The probe also restricts to a single binary decision (stay home or go out) on a single 100-agent population. Whether the provider-level patterns documented here generalize to other decision domains or to larger and differently-distributed agent populations is an open question.

The keyword dictionaries used for §4.3.1–§4.3.3 (defined in §3.7.3) were curated by manual sample inspection rather than learned from the data. While this matches conventions in content-analysis research, an unsupervised approach (for example, topic modeling on the response embeddings) might surface decision themes the manual approach misses.

Lastly, the probe evaluates general-purpose chat models with light persona prompts. Whether richer interview-style prompting park2024_1000, fine-tuning on human-behavior traces kolluri2025, or purpose-built behavioral models simile_ai would reduce the model-to-model variability documented here is left as future work.

6. Conclusion

This study probed 21 LLM configurations from three providers across 100 frozen personas, 40 infection levels, and 5 repetitions per cell, totaling 420,000 binary decisions, to ask how the choice of LLM shapes generative-agent behavior. Mobility-curve, persona, and response findings all varied across LLMs. The model attributes a researcher would typically use to choose an LLM (size, generation, knowledge cutoff, release date) did not predict that variation. Across LLMs the direction of trait effects agreed; magnitude varied several-fold by provider. The reasoning text turned out to encode predictive signal beyond the decision itself, revealing an other-focused-vs-self-focused framing of the stay-home choice.

GABM was introduced to replace rule-based decision-making with LLM-mediated decisions ghaffarzadegan2024. Human behavior resists clean mathematical specification: how the death of a grandparent during an epidemic alters an agent’s mobility, for instance, is hard to derive from a rule. By connecting agents to a well-trained LLM, the modeler offloads that specification problem. The variation across LLMs observed here means that move has a hidden cost: the LLM choice itself becomes the parameterization. The implication is not that practitioners should pick an LLM that produces a desired behavioral profile; that would simply re-introduce the parameter-tuning subjectivity GABM was meant to escape. The implication is that GABM, as currently constituted with general-purpose LLMs, is most defensible for qualitative claims (which agree across LLMs) and weakest for quantitative claims (which vary several-fold by provider).

Purpose-built models are emerging in response. Recent work fine-tunes general-purpose LLMs on real human-action traces to improve behavioral-simulation accuracy kolluri2025, and well-funded efforts like Simile.ai simile_ai are building LLMs specifically to capture the population-level diversity of human decision-making rather than the single, polished persona of a chat assistant. Whether magnitude reliability emerges as general-purpose models scale, or whether it requires this kind of specialized training, is an open question this study cannot answer. Until then, GABM practitioners should treat the LLM choice as the central parameter in their model and report LLM sensitivity alongside any quantitative claim.

Modelers from epidemiology, sociology, political science, economics, and complex-systems modeling could extend the probe approach to their own decision domains, and LLM-sensitivity testing could be treated as a routine part of any GABM publication’s evidentiary base, much as classical agent-based modeling has long been expected to demonstrate sensitivity to parameter choices.
